# Chapter 6. Adverbs

## What you will learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- explain what adverbs modify and recognize common adverb types;
- place adverbs naturally in sentences and notice how position changes emphasis or meaning;
- distinguish adverbs from adjectives, including common pairs such as *hard/hardly* and *late/lately*; and
- use intensifiers and downtoners selectively for a clear, appropriate tone.

## 6.1 What is an adverb?

An **adverb** modifies a verb, an adjective, another adverb, or sometimes a whole clause. It can tell how, when, where, how often, or to what degree something happens.

- Verb: The assistant answered **quickly**. (*Quickly* modifies *answered*.)
- Adjective: The instructions were **very** clear. (*Very* modifies *clear*.)
- Another adverb: The bus arrived **quite** late. (*Quite* modifies *late*.)
- Whole clause: **Fortunately**, we had saved a backup. (*Fortunately* comments on the whole statement.)

Many adverbs end in *-ly*, but plenty do not: *often, soon, here, there, well, fast,* and *hard* can be adverbs. Also, not every *-ly* word is an adverb. *Friendly, lively,* and *costly* are usually adjectives:

> The **friendly** receptionist spoke **politely**.

*Friendly* describes the noun *receptionist*; *politely* modifies the verb *spoke*. Identify the job a word does rather than relying on its spelling.

## 6.2 Common types of adverbs

Adverbs are often grouped by the kind of information they add. A single adverb may fit more than one category depending on how it is used.

### Manner: how?

Adverbs of **manner** describe how an action is performed.

> She explained the process **clearly**.
>
> Please handle the equipment **carefully**.

Many manner adverbs are formed by adding *-ly* to an adjective: *careful/carefully, quiet/quietly, complete/completely*. Spelling may change: *easy/easily, basic/basically*. Some common adverbs have the same form as their adjectives: *fast, hard,* and *straight*.

### Time: when?

Adverbs of **time** tell when something happens or how long it lasts.

> We will meet **tomorrow**.
>
> The office is **currently** closed.
>
> Please wait **briefly**.

Common time adverbs include *now, soon, yesterday, already, still, yet, recently,* and *later*.

### Place: where?

Adverbs of **place** tell where something happens or in what direction.

> The team works **upstairs**.
>
> Please move the chairs **outside**.
>
> We searched **everywhere**.

Common examples include *here, there, nearby, away, indoors, upstairs,* and *forward*.

### Frequency: how often?

Adverbs of **frequency** say how often something happens.

> I **usually** take the train.
>
> The printer **rarely** jams.
>
> They meet **once a week**.

Common frequency adverbs include *always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, seldom,* and *never*. Frequency phrases such as *once a week* and *every month* also answer “how often?”

### Degree: how much or to what extent?

Adverbs of **degree** show intensity, extent, or amount. They commonly modify adjectives and other adverbs, and some also modify verbs.

> The instructions are **extremely** detailed.
>
> The report is **almost** complete.
>
> I **strongly** recommend a backup.

Common degree adverbs include *very, quite, rather, almost, nearly, completely, somewhat, slightly, too,* and *enough*. The position of *enough* differs from many degree adverbs: it usually follows an adjective or adverb (*clear enough, quickly enough*).

## 6.3 Where adverbs appear

Adverbs can appear in more than one position. Choose a position that sounds natural and makes the intended meaning clear.

### Manner adverbs

Manner adverbs often appear after the verb or after the verb's object:

> The speaker answered **calmly**.
>
> The speaker answered the question **calmly**.

Do not usually put a manner adverb between a verb and its object:

> Natural: She completed the form **carefully**.
>
> Less natural: She completed **carefully** the form.

If you want to emphasize how the action was done, a manner adverb can sometimes begin the sentence:

> **Carefully**, she placed the signed form in the folder.

### Frequency adverbs

Adverbs such as *always, usually, often,* and *never* normally appear before a main verb:

> We **often review** the schedule.
>
> He **never misses** a deadline.

They usually follow a form of *be*:

> She **is usually** available after lunch.

When a helping verb is present, the frequency adverb usually comes after the first helping verb:

> We **have often discussed** this issue.
>
> The team **can usually finish** by Friday.

Some frequency expressions commonly appear at the end of a sentence: *We meet **once a week***.

### Time and place adverbs

Time and place adverbs often appear at the beginning or end of a sentence:

> We will review the draft **tomorrow**.
>
> **Tomorrow**, we will review the draft.
>
> The participants are waiting **outside**.

When both place and manner information appear, place often comes before manner:

> She spoke **in the meeting room** **quietly**.

Writers may change the order to emphasize a particular detail. If a sentence feels crowded, move one detail to the beginning or make it a separate sentence.

### Degree adverbs

Degree adverbs usually appear directly before the adjective or adverb they modify:

> **very** helpful
>
> **almost** finished
>
> **remarkably** clearly

Some degree adverbs modify verbs and usually come before the main verb or after a helping verb:

> I **really appreciate** the update.
>
> We **have completely revised** the introduction.

### Position can change the meaning

Some adverbs—especially *only, just, even,* and *almost*—can change what a sentence emphasizes depending on where they appear.

> **Only Maya** reviewed the report. (No one else reviewed it.)
>
> Maya **only reviewed** the report. (She reviewed it but did not do more, such as edit or approve it.)
>
> Maya reviewed **only the report**. (She did not review the other materials.)

For clearer writing, place the adverb next to the word or phrase it is meant to limit. Similar care helps with *almost*:

> **Almost every student** submitted the form. (Nearly every student did.)
>
> Every student **almost submitted** the form. (They came close to submitting it, but did not.)

The second sentence is often not the intended meaning. Moving *almost* prevents confusion.

## 6.4 Adjectives and adverbs

Adjectives describe nouns or pronouns; adverbs commonly describe verbs. Some adverbs are formed from adjectives with *-ly*, but there are important exceptions and pairs with different meanings.

### *Good* and *well*

Use **good** as an adjective to describe a noun or pronoun:

> She wrote a **good** summary.
>
> The summary looks **good**.

Use **well** as an adverb to describe how an action is done:

> She writes **well**.
>
> The team worked **well** together.

*Well* can also be an adjective meaning “in good health”:

> I feel **well** today.

### *Hard* and *hardly*

**Hard** can be an adjective or an adverb. As an adverb, it means “with effort” or “with force.” **Hardly** means “almost not” or “barely”—it does not mean “with effort.”

> They worked **hard** to meet the deadline.
>
> We could **hardly** hear the announcement.

### *Late* and *lately*

**Late** means “after the expected time” or “near the end of a period.” **Lately** means “recently.”

> The client arrived **late**.
>
> Have you spoken with the client **lately**?

### Other common pairs

- **high** describes a position or a large level; **highly** often means “very” or “to a high degree”: *The shelf is high; a highly respected researcher.*
- **near** can be an adjective, preposition, or adverb; **nearly** means “almost”: *The deadline is near; we nearly finished on time.*
- **fast** can be both an adjective and an adverb: *a fast connection; the connection runs fast.* Do not add *-ly* to mean speed: *fast*, not *fastly*.

Check the intended meaning when a pair differs. Adding *-ly* does not always create a predictable version of the original word.

## 6.5 Intensifiers and downtoners

An **intensifier** raises the strength of a description or statement. Common intensifiers include *very, really, extremely, completely, absolutely,* and *highly*.

> The revised plan is **very clear**.
>
> We **strongly recommend** saving a backup.

A **downtoner** lowers or softens the strength of a statement. Common downtoners include *fairly, somewhat, slightly, rather, almost,* and *partly*.

> The first draft is **somewhat unclear**.
>
> The room is **slightly warmer** than expected.

Intensifiers and downtoners can help express degree, certainty, or diplomacy. Too many can weaken writing because they repeat an idea, make the tone sound exaggerated, or hide the evidence for a claim:

> Inflated: The new process is **really very extremely efficient**.
>
> Clearer: The new process **reduces processing time by 20 percent**.

When you can, replace a stack of intensifiers with a precise adjective, a concrete fact, or a direct statement. In academic or professional writing, make the strength of a claim match the available evidence.

## 6.6 Common learner challenges

### Assuming every *-ly* word is an adverb

*Friendly* and *costly* are usually adjectives. Check whether the word describes a noun or modifies an action.

### Assuming every adverb ends in *-ly*

Words such as *often, soon, hard,* and *fast* can be adverbs. Look at their function.

### Confusing adjective and adverb forms

Use an adjective after a linking verb to describe the subject (*The explanation sounds clear*). Use an adverb to modify an action (*She explained it clearly*).

### Using *hardly* to mean “with effort”

Write *They worked hard*, not *They worked hardly*. *Hardly* means “almost not.”

### Putting an adverb too far from what it modifies

Move words such as *only* and *almost* beside the intended word or phrase. This avoids accidental changes in meaning.

### Overusing intensifiers

Remove repeated degree words and choose one accurate description. If possible, support a strong claim with a measurable detail.

## 6.8 Answer key

### A. Place the adverb

1. **She carefully completed the online application.** / **She completed the online application carefully.** The adverb modifies *completed*.
2. **We often discussed the project timeline.** The frequency adverb normally comes before the main verb.
3. **The new staff member is usually available on Thursdays.** The frequency adverb follows the linking verb *is*.
4. **I have read almost every chapter in the handbook.** *Almost* modifies *every chapter*, meaning nearly all chapters are finished. The original placement can suggest that the reader nearly performed the action of reading, but did not.
5. **Only Luis can approve the request.** Placing *only* before *Luis* means no one else can approve it.
6. **The guests waited quietly outside.** Place usually precedes manner here. **The guests waited outside quietly** is also possible, with a different rhythm or emphasis.

### B. Choose the correct form or meaning

1. **clearly** — It modifies the action *explained*.
2. **simple** — It describes the instructions after the linking verb *look*.
3. **hard** — It means “with effort.”
4. **hardly** — It means “almost not.”
5. **late** — The speaker arrived after the expected time.
6. **lately** — It means “recently.”
7. **fast** — This word can be an adverb without *-ly*.
8. **highly** — It means “to a high degree.”
9. **wonderful** — It describes the food after the linking verb *smells*.
10. **politely** — It modifies the action *responded*.

### C. What does the adverb modify?

1. **Fortunately** modifies the whole clause *the clinic had an open appointment*; it comments on the statement.
2. **Rarely** modifies *cancels*; it gives frequency information.
3. **There** modifies *place* by telling where; it gives place information.
4. **Nearly** modifies *missed* or, more precisely, the event of missing the deadline; it indicates degree or near-completion.
5. **Carefully** modifies *checked*; it gives manner information.
6. **Tomorrow** modifies *will meet* by telling when; it gives time information.

### D. Edit for precision

Answers will vary. One possible revision is:

> The new scheduling tool is **useful**. It works **quickly** and automatically sends reminders to staff. The manager **barely** reviewed the settings, so **nearly every reminder went out at the wrong time**. The team is testing it **carefully** and expects to finish the review soon.

This version removes the stack *really very extremely*, uses *quickly* to modify *works*, places *automatically* before the verb phrase it modifies, and replaces *hardly* with *barely* to make the intended meaning explicit: the manager did very little review. *Probably most likely* repeats the same uncertainty, so the revision keeps one clear expression. It also places *nearly* before *every reminder* to show that almost all reminders went out at the wrong time. Placing it before *sent* would suggest that the sending almost happened but did not.

## Chapter summary

Adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, other adverbs, or whole clauses. They commonly express manner, time, place, frequency, or degree. Many end in *-ly*, but many do not, and some *-ly* words are adjectives. Position depends on the adverb's type and what it modifies; words such as *only* and *almost* can change a sentence's meaning when moved. Adjectives describe nouns or pronouns, while adverbs often modify actions: a *good* result, a result explained *well*. Some pairs have distinct meanings: *hard* means “with effort,” while *hardly* means “almost not”; *late* means “after the expected time,” while *lately* means “recently.” Intensifiers and downtoners express degree, but precise wording is usually stronger than a stack of emphasis words.
