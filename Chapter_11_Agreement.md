# Chapter 11. Agreement

## What you will learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- explain grammatical agreement and find the subject that controls a verb;
- match subjects and verbs in common sentence patterns, including questions and *there is/are* statements;
- handle compound subjects, intervening phrases, indefinite pronouns, collective nouns, and expressions of amount;
- match pronouns to clear antecedents in number and person; and
- revise agreement errors in emails and short reports, explaining what controls each choice.

## 11.1 What is agreement?

**Agreement** is the matching of grammatical forms that belong together. In English, the most visible kinds are **subject-verb agreement** and **pronoun-antecedent agreement**.

- Subject-verb: **The analyst checks** the figures. / **The analysts check** the figures.
- Pronoun-antecedent: **Mina** brought **her** notebook. / **The students** brought **their** notebooks.

The form of the verb or pronoun gives readers information about the subject or the noun the pronoun refers to. Agreement can be straightforward, but long phrases, questions, and words with special meanings can make the controlling subject hard to see.

## 11.2 Subject-verb agreement

In the simple present tense, most English verbs add *-s* or *-es* with a singular third-person subject (*he, she, it,* or a singular noun). Most plural subjects take the base form. The verb *be* has several different forms.

| Subject | Present-tense verb | Example |
|---|---|---|
| I | *work* | I **work** in the admissions office. |
| You | *work* | You **work** with the design team. |
| He/she/it; one person or thing | *works* | She **works** on campus. |
| We/they; plural nouns | *work* | The advisers **work** on campus. |
| I | *am* | I **am** ready. |
| He/she/it; singular noun | *is* | The office **is** open. |
| You/we/they; plural nouns | *are* | The offices **are** open. |

In the past tense, most verbs have the same form with singular and plural subjects (*The student called; The students called*). The verb *be* still changes (*was/were*).

> Singular: The manager **was** available.
>
> Plural: The managers **were** available.

### Find the true subject

The **subject** is the person or thing the sentence is about or, in an action sentence, the one that performs the action. The subject—not a nearby noun—controls the verb.

> **The list** of available courses **is** online.

The subject is *list*, not *courses*. The phrase *of available courses* adds information about the list; it does not control the verb.

A useful method:

1. Find the sentence's verb or verb phrase.
2. Ask who or what does the action, or who or what the sentence describes.
3. Identify the subject's main noun or pronoun.
4. Decide whether the subject is singular or plural.
5. Match the verb to that subject, ignoring nouns inside intervening phrases.

> **The results** from the second survey **show** a different pattern.

The subject is *results* (plural), so the verb is *show*. The singular noun *survey* is inside a modifying phrase and does not control the verb.

### Singular and plural nouns

Plural nouns usually take plural verbs; singular nouns usually take singular verbs:

> **A student needs** a campus card.
>
> **Students need** campus cards.

Be careful with nouns ending in *-s* that are singular in meaning:

> **The news is** encouraging.
>
> **Economics is** required for this program. (The subject is the academic field.)

Some nouns that name paired items take plural verbs (*The scissors are on the desk*). A phrase such as *a pair of scissors* has the singular subject *pair*: *A pair of scissors is on the desk.*

## 11.3 Compound subjects

A **compound subject** has two or more subjects joined together.

### Subjects joined by *and*

Two subjects joined by *and* usually take a plural verb:

> **The tutor and the student are** meeting today.

If the two nouns name one unit, one dish, one title, or one combined idea, a singular verb may be more natural:

> **Macaroni and cheese is** on today's menu. (One dish.)
>
> **Research and development is** a department in this company. (One combined function.)

When each subject is introduced by *each* or *every*, a singular verb is common:

> **Every student and adviser receives** a copy.

### Subjects joined by *or* or *nor*

With *or, nor, either/or,* and *neither/nor*, the verb often agrees with the subject closest to it. If this makes the sentence awkward, rewrite it.

> **Either the supervisor or the assistants are** leading the tour.
>
> **Either the assistants or the supervisor is** leading the tour.

The nearer subject controls the verb in these examples (*assistants*; *supervisor*). A clearer rewrite can avoid the shift: *The supervisor will lead the tour, or the assistants will.*

### Subjects joined by *as well as* or *along with*

Phrases such as *as well as, along with,* and *together with* do not normally create a compound subject. The first subject still controls the verb:

> **The project lead, along with two interns, is** presenting the results.

The phrase *along with two interns* adds information; it does not change the singular subject *project lead* to plural.

## 11.4 Intervening phrases and clauses

Words between a subject and its verb can distract from the agreement choice. Find the main subject and set the extra phrase aside:

> The application, **including the two required references, is** due on Friday.
>
> The students **who attend the evening class are** eligible for tutoring.

The subject in the first sentence is *application*. In the second, it is *students*. The nearby nouns *references* and *class* do not control the main verb.

### Expressions beginning with *one of*

In *one of the applicants*, the subject of the main sentence is *one*, so the main verb is singular:

> **One of the applicants has** already accepted.

In a longer sentence, identify which noun the verb belongs to:

> **One of the applicants who have completed the interview** is returning today.

The main clause subject is *one*, so *is returning* is singular. The relative clause *who have completed the interview* refers to *applicants*, so *have* is plural.

## 11.5 Indefinite pronouns and other common subjects

Some indefinite pronouns are grammatically singular, some are plural, and some depend on the noun or meaning that follows.

### Usually singular

These common indefinite pronouns usually take singular verbs:

> **Everyone is** invited.
>
> **Each has** a different number.
>
> **Neither of the options works** for me.

Common singular indefinite pronouns include *anyone, anybody, anything, everyone, everybody, everything, someone, somebody, something, no one, nobody, nothing, each, either,* and *neither*.

### Usually plural

These common indefinite pronouns usually take plural verbs:

> **Both are** available.
>
> **Few have** responded.
>
> **Several were** missing.

Common plural forms include *both, few, many,* and *several* when they stand alone as subjects.

### Depends on meaning or the noun in the phrase

Words such as *all, any, more, most,* and *some* can take singular or plural verbs depending on what they refer to:

> **Some of the information is** missing. (*Information* is noncount.)
>
> **Some of the forms are** missing. (*Forms* is plural count.)

Similarly:

> **All of the equipment is** ready.
>
> **All of the employees are** ready.

## 11.6 Collective nouns

A **collective noun** names a group, such as *team, staff, family, committee,* or *audience*. In American English, collective nouns commonly take a singular verb when the group acts as one unit:

> **The team is** reviewing the proposal.
>
> **The committee has** reached a decision.

Some varieties of English, especially British English, more often use a plural verb when the writer emphasizes the group members as individuals. Usage also varies with the noun and context. To make the intended meaning clear, use a plural noun phrase when focusing on members:

> **The team members are** reviewing separate sections.

Keep the choice consistent within a passage unless the meaning changes. If your institution or publication has a style preference, follow it.

## 11.7 Questions and inverted word order

In questions, the subject often follows a helping verb. Do not mistake a nearby noun for the subject.

> **Does** the new schedule **include** evening classes?
>
> **Do** the new schedules **include** evening classes?
>
> **Has** the package of documents **arrived**?

The subjects are *schedule, schedules,* and *package*. The main verbs after *does* and *do* use the base form (*include*), while the helping verbs agree with the subject.

To check a question, turn it into a statement:

> **Are** the updated figures available? → The updated figures **are** available.

Some statements also place the verb before the subject, especially after *here* or *there*:

> Here **is** the file you requested.
>
> Here **are** the files you requested.

Find the subject after the verb and match the form.

## 11.8 *There is* and *there are*

In sentences that begin with **there is/are**, *there* is not the subject. The noun phrase that follows the verb controls agreement:

> **There is** one seat near the window.
>
> **There are** four seats near the window.

In questions, the same agreement applies:

> **Is there** a quiet place to study?
>
> **Are there** any quiet places to study?

With a compound subject, the verb form may depend on the order and style, but plural agreement is usually expected when the subject includes plural nouns:

> **There are** a printer and two scanners in the workroom.

For a smoother sentence, put the subject first: *A printer and two scanners are in the workroom.*

In casual speech, some speakers use *there's* before a plural list (*There's two forms on the desk*). In standard edited writing, use *there are* with a plural subject: *There are two forms on the desk.*

## 11.9 Expressions of amount and meaning-based agreement

Some subjects name amounts of time, money, distance, or measurement. If the amount is treated as one total or one unit, use a singular verb:

> **Three hours is** enough for the workshop. (One block of time.)
>
> **Fifty dollars is** the registration fee. (One total amount.)
>
> **Ten kilometers is** too far to walk. (One distance.)

Use a plural verb when the sentence emphasizes individual units or items:

> **Three hours have** passed since the workshop began. (The hours are counted as units of time.)
>
> **Fifty dollars were** scattered across the table. (Separate bills or coins.)

Fractions and percentages usually agree with the noun in an *of*-phrase:

> **Half of the equipment is** ready.
>
> **Half of the participants are** ready.
>
> **Thirty percent of the budget is** reserved for travel.
>
> **Thirty percent of the applicants are** international students.

Agreement follows the meaning of the quantity and the noun it measures. If the intended meaning is not obvious, rewrite the sentence to name the unit or items directly.

## 11.10 Pronoun-antecedent agreement

A **pronoun antecedent** is the noun or noun phrase that a pronoun refers to. The pronoun should match its antecedent in number and person, and the relationship should be clear.

> **Mina** finished **her** report.
>
> **The interns** submitted **their** reports.

The pronoun *her* refers to the singular antecedent *Mina*. The pronoun *their* refers to the plural antecedent *interns*.

### Match the pronoun to a clear antecedent

Avoid using a pronoun when more than one noun could be its antecedent:

> Unclear: The supervisor spoke with the assistant after **she** reviewed the file.

Who reviewed the file—the supervisor or the assistant? Repeat the intended noun or restructure:

> The supervisor spoke with the assistant after **the assistant** reviewed the file.
>
> After reviewing the file, the supervisor spoke with the assistant.

Clear reference is more important than avoiding every repeated noun. Chapter 3 covers pronoun reference in more detail.

### Pronouns with indefinite antecedents

Singular indefinite antecedents such as *everyone, someone, each,* and *neither* traditionally take singular pronouns in formal grammar. Contemporary English also uses **singular they** when the person's gender is unknown, unspecified, or when an individual uses *they* as a personal pronoun:

> **Everyone** should bring **their** identification.
>
> **A student** can ask the adviser if **they** need help.
>
> **Alex** said **they** will send the notes.

With *they* for one person, use the usual *they* verb forms: *they are, they have, they do*. The verb that agrees directly with *everyone* remains singular: *Everyone **is** invited.*

When a sentence becomes hard to follow, rephrase with a plural antecedent:

> Each applicant should bring **their** portfolio.
>
> **Applicants** should bring **their** portfolios.

Use a person's stated pronouns when known, and do not guess. See Chapter 3 for more on pronoun forms and respectful reference.

### Compound antecedents

Two antecedents joined by *and* generally take a plural pronoun:

> **The manager and the assistant** shared **their** notes.

If the nouns are joined by *or* or *nor*, the pronoun usually agrees with the nearer antecedent. Rewriting can avoid an awkward or unclear result:

> Either **the manager** or **the assistants** can share **their** notes.
>
> The manager can share the notes, or the assistants can share them.

## 11.11 Common agreement problems

### Letting a nearby noun control the verb

Find the subject, not just the noun closest to the verb:

> Incorrect: The schedule of workshops **include** two evening sessions.
>
> Correct: The schedule of workshops **includes** two evening sessions.

### Forgetting the singular *-s* in the present tense

> Incorrect: The device **need** a new battery.
>
> Correct: The device **needs** a new battery.

### Using a plural verb with *everyone* or *each*

> Incorrect: Everyone **have** a copy.
>
> Correct: Everyone **has** a copy.

The pronoun after *everyone* may be singular *they*, but the verb that agrees with *everyone* is singular.

### Treating *there* as the subject

> Incorrect: There **is** three rooms available.
>
> Correct: There **are** three rooms available.

The subject is *three rooms*.

### Mixing singular and plural agreement in a collective noun

Choose agreement based on the group meaning and variety of English, then keep it consistent. To avoid uncertainty, name the members when the individuals matter: *The committee members are reviewing separate submissions.*

## 11.13 Answer key

### A. Correct agreement in an email

> **Subject: Details for Friday's workshop**
>
> Hi team,
>
> The list of materials **is** attached. Each of the presenters **has** a copy, and both of the room coordinators **are** available to help. There **are** two boxes of name badges at reception. The event begins at 9:00, but neither the screen nor the speakers **are** working. The final room assignments **were** included in the email. Everyone should bring **their** laptop if **they** plan to show slides. A few of the volunteers **have** offered to help, and the group members are meeting at 8:30 to set up.
>
> Thanks,
> Noor

The eight corrections are:

1. *are → is* — *List* is the singular subject; *of materials* is an intervening phrase.
2. *have → has* — *Each* is singular.
3. *is → are* — *Both* is plural.
4. *is → are* — The subject after *there* is *two boxes*, which is plural.
5. *was → were* — The subject *assignments* is plural.
6. *his → their* — *Everyone* is a singular indefinite antecedent; singular *they* is used inclusively.
7. *plans → plan* — The antecedent for *they* is singular *everyone*, but the pronoun *they* takes the usual base-form verb in the present: *they plan*.
8. *has → have* — *A few* is plural.

The phrase *neither the screen nor the speakers are* is already correct: with *neither/nor*, the verb agrees with the nearer subject, *speakers*.

### B. Correct agreement in a short report

1. **The number of applications has increased since May.** *Number* is the singular subject; *of applications* modifies it.
2. **Several of the forms were missing a signature.** *Several* refers to plural forms.
3. **Neither of the proposed schedules meets the team's needs.** *Neither* is singular in this construction.
4. **The team, along with two consultants, is presenting its findings.** *Team* is treated as one unit; *along with two consultants* does not make the subject plural. If emphasizing people, write **The team members, along with two consultants, are presenting their findings.**
5. **There are several reasons for the delay.** The subject following *there* is *several reasons*.
6. **Four hours is enough to complete the training.** The time is treated as one total period.
7. **Every participant should check their registration confirmation.** *Every participant* is grammatically singular; singular *they/their* is used for a person of unknown or unspecified gender. The verb form *should check* is controlled by modal *should*, which is followed by the base verb.
8. **The analyst or the assistants will update their sections of the report.** *Their* clearly refers to the plural *assistants*, but the original does not reveal who is updating which section. A clearer rewrite is **The analyst will update one section of the report, or the assistants will update their sections.**

### C. Explain the choice

1. **are** — The nearer subject, *designers*, is plural.
2. **needs** — *Equipment* is noncount and singular in this meaning.
3. **need** — *Monitors* is plural.
4. **was** — *News* is singular in form and agreement.
5. **Does** — *Each employee* is singular.
6. **their** — Singular *they/their* provides an inclusive, clear form without assuming a person's gender. The subject *a student or a tutor* is a combined alternative; the verb after *should* remains the base form *bring*.

### D. Apply it

Answers will vary. Check that the true subject is identified, that intervening phrases do not distract from it, and that pronouns point clearly to antecedents. Agreement should reflect the grammatical structure unless a collective noun or amount is being treated according to its intended meaning.

## Chapter summary

Agreement matches grammatical forms that belong together. Find the true subject before choosing a verb, and do not let a nearby noun inside a phrase control it. Compound subjects, indefinite pronouns, collective nouns, questions, *there is/are* statements, and expressions of amount each have common patterns. Pronouns should match their antecedents in person and number, and readers should be able to tell what each pronoun refers to. Singular *they* can refer to one person; the verb that agrees with the pronoun follows the usual *they* form (*they are, they have, they do*). In some cases, such as collective nouns or amounts of time and money, agreement reflects whether the writer means one unit or individual members. When a sentence becomes awkward or unclear, rewrite it to make the controlling subject or antecedent explicit.
