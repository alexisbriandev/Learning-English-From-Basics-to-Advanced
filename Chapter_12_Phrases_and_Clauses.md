# Chapter 12. Phrases and Clauses

## What you will learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- distinguish phrases from clauses by looking for a subject and verb;
- recognize prepositional, verb, noun, adjective, and adverb phrases;
- distinguish independent clauses from dependent clauses;
- identify noun, relative (adjective), and adverb clauses;
- use restrictive and nonrestrictive relative clauses with appropriate commas;
- add detail and combine ideas without making sentences confusing; and
- repair fragments and misplaced or dangling modifiers.

## 12.1 Phrases and clauses: the basic difference

A **phrase** is a group of related words that functions as a unit but does not contain a complete subject-verb combination. A **clause** is a group of words that has a subject and a verb.

> **after the meeting** (phrase; no subject-verb combination)
>
> **the meeting ended** (clause; *meeting* is the subject and *ended* is the verb)

A phrase can add detail to a sentence, but it is not usually a complete sentence by itself. A clause may be independent or dependent, as explained later in this chapter.

### Finding subjects and verbs

To decide whether a word group is a clause, identify its subject and verb. The subject tells who or what the clause is about; the verb expresses an action, event, state, or relationship.

> **when the office closes**

The subject is *office*; the verb is *closes*. This is a clause, even though it cannot stand alone as a complete sentence in this use.

> **after the office closes**

This whole word group is a prepositional phrase beginning with *after*. Inside it, *the office closes* is a clause that serves as the object of the preposition. A phrase can contain a clause, so the distinction is about the word group's overall structure and job.

## 12.2 Common phrases

Phrases are named by their main word or their function. A phrase does not have to be long: *in June, very useful,* and *has been working* are all phrases.

### Prepositional phrases

A **prepositional phrase** begins with a preposition and includes its object and any words that describe the object.

> **in the quiet study room**
>
> **with two colleagues**
>
> **before the instructor arrives**

In *with two colleagues*, *with* is the preposition and *two colleagues* is its object. A prepositional phrase can modify a noun or a verb:

> The **report on campus housing** is ready. (*On campus housing* describes the report.)
>
> We met **after class**. (*After class* tells when we met.)

Prepositional phrases are not clauses by themselves, but their object may be a clause in some patterns: *before the instructor arrives*. The group as a whole begins with the preposition *before*.

### Verb phrases

A **verb phrase** consists of a main verb, sometimes together with one or more helping verbs. The main verb carries the central meaning; helping verbs express tense, aspect, voice, questions, or other grammatical meanings.

> **submitted** the application
>
> **has submitted** the application
>
> **may have been reviewing** the application

In *may have been reviewing*, *reviewing* is the main verb; *may, have,* and *been* are helping verbs. A verb phrase alone is not a clause because it does not include a subject:

> **has been reviewing** (verb phrase)
>
> **The committee has been reviewing** (clause: subject + verb phrase)

### Noun phrases

A **noun phrase** is a group of words organized around a noun or pronoun. It can act as a subject, object, or complement in a clause.

> **The new student in my seminar** asked a question.
>
> I reviewed **the draft you sent yesterday**.

The first noun phrase is the subject of *asked*. The second is the object of *reviewed*. A noun phrase may include a relative clause, as in *the draft you sent yesterday*.

### Adjective phrases

An **adjective phrase** is a group of words organized around an adjective. It describes a noun or pronoun, or follows a linking verb to describe the subject.

> a **very useful** reference
>
> The reference is **useful for beginners**.

In *very useful*, *useful* is the main adjective and *very* modifies it. In *useful for beginners*, the prepositional phrase adds information to the adjective.

### Adverb phrases

An **adverb phrase** is a group of words that functions like an adverb. It modifies a verb, adjective, another adverb, or a whole clause.

> She answered **very confidently**.
>
> We arrived **quite early**.
>
> The report is due **later this week**.

The phrases *very confidently* and *quite early* describe how or when an action happened; *later this week* tells when the report is due.

### A quick phrase check

For each group, ask what its main word is and what job the whole group does:

> **with careful attention** — prepositional phrase; adds information about how something is done.
>
> **extremely useful** — adjective phrase; describes a noun or pronoun.
>
> **will be arriving soon** — verb phrase plus an adverb; the verb phrase is *will be arriving*.

## 12.3 Independent and dependent clauses

An **independent clause** has a subject and a verb and can stand as a complete sentence:

> **The seminar ended.**

A **dependent clause** also has a subject and a verb, but it begins with a word or follows a pattern that makes it depend on another clause to complete the thought:

> **because the seminar ended**

The dependent clause tells a reason, but in this use it leaves the reader expecting a result. Join it to an independent clause:

> **Because the seminar ended**, we went to lunch.
>
> We went to lunch **because the seminar ended**.

The first sentence places the dependent clause first and uses a comma after it. The second places it after the independent clause; a comma is usually not needed. Chapter 14 covers punctuation patterns in more detail.

### Common signals of dependent clauses

Dependent clauses often begin with a subordinating conjunction or a relative word:

- Subordinating conjunctions: *because, although, if, when, while, before, after, unless, since*.
- Relative words: *who, whom, whose, which, that, where,* and *when*.
- Noun-clause signals: *that, whether, if,* and question words such as *what, who, why,* and *how*.

The signal word helps identify the clause's role, but it does not always make a clause dependent. For example, *that* can be a determiner (*that report*) or a pronoun (*that is mine*) as well as a clause signal (*that the report was late*).

## 12.4 Noun clauses

A **noun clause** is a dependent clause that functions like a noun. It can be a subject, an object, or a complement in a sentence.

### Noun clause as a subject

> **What the committee decides** will affect the schedule.

The clause *what the committee decides* is the subject of *will affect*.

### Noun clause as an object

> I know **that the room is available**.
>
> She asked **whether the office was open**.
>
> We discussed **how the new process works**.

The clauses beginning with *that, whether,* and *how* act as objects of *know, asked,* and *discussed*.

### Noun clause as a complement

> The question is **whether we have enough time**.

The clause *whether we have enough time* completes the meaning of *the question is*.

In some object clauses, *that* can be left out when the meaning remains clear:

> I think **(that) the proposal is practical**.

Keeping *that* can make a long sentence easier to follow or clarify where one clause begins.

## 12.5 Relative (adjective) clauses

A **relative clause**, also called an **adjective clause**, describes or identifies a noun or pronoun. It often begins with a relative pronoun or adverb:

- *who* or *whom* for people;
- *whose* for possession or association;
- *which* or *that* for things and animals in many contexts;
- *where* for places; and
- *when* for times.

> The advisor **who helped me** works on the second floor.
>
> I returned the laptop **that I borrowed**.
>
> We visited the office **where my sister works**.

The relative clause follows the noun it describes. In the first sentence, *who helped me* identifies or describes *advisor*.

### Restrictive relative clauses: information needed to identify the noun

A **restrictive** (or **essential**) relative clause identifies which person or thing the writer means. Do not set it off with commas:

> Employees **who work remotely** can join the meeting online.

The clause tells us which employees. Without it, the sentence could refer to all employees.

> The document **that contains the budget** is in the shared folder.

The clause identifies which document. In standard edited American English, *that* is common in restrictive clauses about things. *Which* can also appear in restrictive clauses in some varieties and styles, but using *that* for restrictive clauses and *which* for nonrestrictive clauses is a clear system.

If the relative pronoun is the object of the clause, it can sometimes be omitted in a restrictive clause:

> The report **(that) I submitted** is attached.

Here, *I* is the subject and *submitted* is the verb; *that* is the object of *submitted*, so it may be omitted. Do not omit the relative word when it is the subject:

> The report **that explains the new policy** is attached.

Here, *that* is the subject of *explains*.

### Nonrestrictive relative clauses: extra information

A **nonrestrictive** (or **nonessential**) relative clause adds extra information about a noun that is already identified. Set it off with commas:

> Dr. Chen, **who leads the research team**, will present the results.

The name *Dr. Chen* identifies the person; the clause adds information. Removing the clause leaves the main sentence complete: *Dr. Chen will present the results.*

Use *which* or *who* in nonrestrictive relative clauses, not *that* in standard edited usage:

> The new library, **which opened in May**, has more study rooms.

The commas signal that *which opened in May* is extra information, not a condition that identifies which library.

### Restrictive or nonrestrictive? The meaning can change

Compare:

> My colleagues **who work in accounting** reviewed the report. (Only the accounting colleagues reviewed it.)
>
> My colleagues, **who work in accounting**, reviewed the report. (All my colleagues work in accounting; the clause adds information.)

The commas are not merely a pause. They tell the reader whether the clause is needed to identify the noun or provides extra information. Use commas only when the meaning is nonrestrictive.

## 12.6 Adverb clauses

An **adverb clause** is a dependent clause that modifies a verb or a whole clause. It can show time, cause, condition, contrast, purpose, or result.

| Relationship | Common signal words | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Time | *when, after, before, while, until, once* | **When the session ends**, save your notes. |
| Cause | *because, since, as* | We changed rooms **because the projector failed**. |
| Condition | *if, unless* | **If you need more time**, contact the instructor. |
| Contrast | *although, even though, whereas, while* | **Although the file is large**, it downloads quickly. |
| Purpose | *so that* | She wrote down the steps **so that the team could repeat them**. |

When an adverb clause comes first, use a comma after it:

> **If the train is delayed**, we will join online.

When it follows the independent clause, a comma is usually not needed:

> We will join online **if the train is delayed**.

For punctuation with coordinating and subordinating conjunctions, see Chapter 8; for more sentence-boundary guidance, see Chapter 13.

## 12.7 How phrases and clauses add detail

Writers can expand a short sentence in several ways. Each expansion adds a different kind of information.

> **Base sentence:** The team reviewed the proposal.
>
> **Prepositional phrase:** The team reviewed the proposal **after lunch**.
>
> **Adjective phrase:** The **newly formed** team reviewed the proposal.
>
> **Relative clause:** The team **that the dean selected** reviewed the proposal.
>
> **Adverb clause:** The team reviewed the proposal **because the deadline was near**.

Phrases and clauses help combine ideas and vary sentence structure. Use them to clarify relationships, not to make every sentence longer. A reader should still be able to find the main subject and verb.

## 12.8 Fragments and modifier problems

### Fragments

A **sentence fragment** is a word group presented as a complete sentence even though it does not express a complete independent clause. A dependent clause or a phrase can be a fragment.

> Fragment: **Because the meeting was canceled.**
>
> Complete: **Because the meeting was canceled, we rescheduled the review.**

> Fragment: **The proposal on the desk.**
>
> Complete: **The proposal on the desk needs another signature.**

To repair a fragment, attach it to a related independent clause or add the missing subject or verb. In conversation, advertising, or creative writing, fragments may be deliberate. In academic and professional writing, use them only when the effect is intentional and clear.

### Misplaced modifiers

A **modifier** is a word or word group that describes or limits another part of a sentence. A modifier is **misplaced** when it is positioned so that readers may connect it to the wrong word.

> Unclear: I found a schedule **in my email that was outdated**.

Was the email outdated, or the schedule? Move the modifier next to its target or name the target:

> Clear: I found **an outdated schedule** in my email.
>
> Clear: I found a schedule in my email **that was outdated**. (The relative clause now follows *schedule*, though the first revision is even clearer.)

Words such as *only, almost,* and *just* can also be misplaced:

> **Only Kai** reviewed the budget. (No one else reviewed it.)
>
> Kai reviewed **only the budget**. (Kai did not review other materials.)

### Dangling modifiers

A **dangling modifier** does not clearly describe a word in the sentence, often because the person or thing doing the action is missing.

> Dangling: **After reviewing the schedule**, the room was changed.

The room did not review the schedule. Name the person who did:

> Clear: **After reviewing the schedule, the coordinator changed the room.**

Or make the action passive if the person is unknown or unimportant:

> **After the schedule was reviewed, the room was changed.**

When a sentence begins with a modifying phrase or clause, check that the next words identify the person or thing the modifier describes.

## 12.9 Common learner challenges

### Treating every long word group as a clause

A long phrase may have no subject-verb combination: *during the review of the final report*. A short clause may have one: *when we met*. Look for the subject and verb, not the length.

### Assuming a dependent clause is a complete sentence

Words such as *because, although, if,* and *when* often make a clause dependent. Add an independent clause unless you are intentionally using a fragment for effect.

### Confusing a relative clause with a complete sentence

A relative clause usually describes a noun and depends on it: *the colleague who called yesterday*. It is not a complete sentence by itself.

### Using commas that change the meaning of a relative clause

Ask whether the clause identifies which person or thing. If it does, it is restrictive and usually has no commas. If it adds extra information, use commas.

### Letting a modifier describe the wrong word

Put modifiers near the words they describe. In opening phrases, name the person or thing doing the action immediately after the comma.

2. **the office** — subject of *opens*; **the receptionist** — subject of *checks*. Verb forms: *opens, checks*.
3. **We** — subject of *know*; **the workshop** — subject of *begins*. Verbs: *know, begins*.
4. **The forms** — subject of *are*; **that** — subject of *were submitted*. Verb phrases: *were submitted, are*.
5. **the report** — subject of *includes*; **the committee** — subject of *is reviewing*. Verb phrases: *includes, is reviewing*.

### B. Classify the word groups

1. **before the meeting** — Prepositional phrase; *before* is the preposition and *the meeting* is its object. It tells when the package arrived.
2. **has been preparing** — Verb phrase; *preparing* is the main verb and *has been* are helping verbs.
3. **The most experienced applicant** — Noun phrase; it functions as the subject. *Most experienced* is an adjective phrase inside it.
4. **very clear to the new volunteers** — Adjective phrase headed by *clear*; it describes *instructions*. The prepositional phrase *to the new volunteers* completes or extends the adjective's meaning.
5. **much earlier than expected** — Adverb phrase; it modifies *arrived* and tells when. The comparison phrase *than expected* completes the comparison.
6. **Although the room was crowded** — Dependent adverb clause; it has subject *room* and verb *was*, and it signals contrast.
7. **that the schedule had changed** — Noun clause; it is the object of *heard*. Subject: *schedule*; verb phrase: *had changed*.
8. **that you lent me** — Restrictive relative (adjective) clause; it modifies *book*. Subject: *you*; verb: *lent*.

### C. Restrictive or nonrestrictive?

1. **The laptops that need repairs are in the storage room.** No commas: the clause identifies which laptops.
2. **The new library, which opened last month, has a quiet study floor.** Add commas: the new library is already identified; the clause adds information.
3. **My brother, who lives in Taichung, is visiting this weekend.** Add commas because the speaker has one brother and the clause is extra information.
4. **Employees who work the evening shift should use the side entrance.** No commas: the clause identifies which employees.
5. **The schedule, which I sent this morning, includes the room numbers.** Add commas because the context has one schedule and the clause adds information.

### D. Repair fragments and modifiers

Answers may vary. Sample revisions:

1. **Because the campus shuttle was delayed, I arrived after the lecture began.** The dependent clause is joined to an independent clause.
2. **The notes from the instructor about the final exam are on the course site.** The phrase is used as a subject and given a verb.
3. **As I walked into the conference room, I saw that the projector was already on.** The person doing the walking is named.
4. **We reviewed nearly every application before lunch.** *Nearly* is placed next to *every application* to show that almost all were reviewed.
5. **At the meeting, the staff discussed the student request that was submitted last week.** Moving the relative clause beside *request* makes clear what was submitted. If *the meeting* was submitted last week (an unlikely meaning), the sentence would need a different structure.

### E. Expand a simple sentence

Answers will vary. Sample versions:

1. **The researcher presented the findings at the annual meeting.** (*At the annual meeting* is a prepositional phrase.)
2. **The researcher who led the survey presented the findings.** (*Who led the survey* is a relative clause modifying *researcher*.)
3. **Because the deadline was near, the researcher presented the findings.** (*Because the deadline was near* is an adverb clause.)

### F. Apply it

Answers will vary. Check that every clause has a subject and a verb, that dependent clauses connect to an independent clause, and that phrases and modifiers sit near the words they describe.

## Chapter summary

A phrase is a related group of words that functions as a unit but does not contain a complete subject-verb combination. A clause has a subject and a verb. Phrases may be prepositional, verb, noun, adjective, or adverb phrases. Independent clauses can stand alone; dependent clauses need an independent clause to complete the sentence. Noun clauses function like nouns, relative clauses describe nouns, and adverb clauses add relationships such as time, cause, condition, or contrast. Restrictive relative clauses identify a noun and are not set off with commas; nonrestrictive clauses add extra information and are set off with commas. Phrases and clauses help add detail and vary sentences, but fragments, misplaced modifiers, and dangling modifiers can obscure meaning. Find the subject and verb in each clause, and place modifiers so readers can see what they describe.
