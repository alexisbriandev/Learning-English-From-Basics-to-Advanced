# Chapter 7. Prepositions

## What you will learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- explain what prepositions do and identify the parts of a prepositional phrase;
- choose common prepositions for time, place, movement, and other relationships;
- recognize preposition choices that belong to established word partnerships;
- explain how prepositional phrases add information to nouns and verbs; and
- revise common problems, including unnecessary or missing prepositions and confusion among *in, on,* and *at*.

## 7.1 What is a preposition?

A **preposition** shows a relationship between a noun or pronoun and another part of the sentence. The relationship may involve time, place, direction, cause, method, or connection.

> The orientation begins **at nine**.
>
> Your badge is **in the front pocket**.
>
> We walked **toward the station**.

The prepositions *at, in,* and *toward* connect the time, location, or direction to the rest of each sentence.

### Prepositional phrases

A **prepositional phrase** begins with a preposition and includes its **object**—usually a noun, pronoun, or noun phrase—and any words that describe that object.

> **in the main office**
>
> **with her supervisor**
>
> **before the afternoon class**

In *in the main office*, *in* is the preposition, and *the main office* is its object. The object can include a determiner and adjective. A prepositional phrase does not usually express a complete subject-and-verb idea by itself.

Pronouns after prepositions use object forms:

> Please sit **beside me**.
>
> The invitation came **from them**.

Some prepositions are followed by an *-ing* form:

> She is interested **in learning** more.
>
> We left **after finishing** the meeting.

In these examples, *learning* and *finishing* name activities and follow the prepositions *in* and *after*.

### Prepositions and words that look like them

A word may be a preposition in one sentence and another class in another sentence:

> We waited **outside the building**. (*Outside* is a preposition with the object *the building*.)
>
> We waited **outside**. (*Outside* is an adverb here; it has no object.)

Likewise, *before* is a preposition in *before lunch*, a conjunction in *before we eat*, and an adverb in *I had seen it before*. Look at what follows the word and how the word connects to the rest of the sentence.

## 7.2 Prepositions of time: *at, on,* and *in*

The prepositions *at, on,* and *in* often describe time. Think of them as moving from a specific point, to a day or date, to a broader period. This is a useful pattern, not a rule that explains every expression.

| Preposition | Common time uses | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| *at* | Clock times; specific points or events | *at 8:30; at noon; at night; at the start of the term* |
| *on* | Days and dates | *on Monday; on October 14; on my first day* |
| *in* | Months, years, seasons, and longer periods | *in April; in 2026; in winter; in the twenty-first century* |

Compare:

> The interview is **at** 2:00 **on** Thursday **in** Room 301.
>
> I started the program **in** August.
>
> We will send the final schedule **on** Friday morning.

### Time expressions without a preposition

Some common expressions do not use *at, on,* or *in*:

- *this morning, next week, last year, every Friday, yesterday, tomorrow*
- *We will meet **next Tuesday**.* (Not *on next Tuesday* in standard edited usage.)
- *I submitted the form **yesterday**.* (Not *on yesterday*.)

Other time expressions have their own patterns:

- *for* + a duration: *for two hours, for three months*;
- *since* + a starting point: *since Monday, since 2022*;
- *by* + a deadline: *by 5:00 p.m.* (no later than that time);
- *until* + an end point: *until 5:00 p.m.* (continuing up to that time);
- *during* + an event or period: *during the seminar, during the summer*;
- *from … to/until* + beginning and end: *from 9:00 to noon*.

> Please send the slides **by Friday**. (Friday is the deadline.)
>
> The office is open **until Friday**. (It remains open up to Friday.)

## 7.3 Prepositions of place and position

Prepositions of place show where someone or something is.

| Preposition | Common relationship | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *at* | A point, address, or activity location | *at the entrance; at 14 Pine Street; at work* |
| *in* | Inside an enclosed space or within an area | *in the office; in the city; in Taiwan* |
| *on* | On a surface, line, floor, or some forms of public transport | *on the desk; on the second floor; on the bus* |
| *under/below* | Lower than something | *under the table; below the window* |
| *above/over* | Higher than something | *above the door; over the city* |
| *behind/in front of* | At the back or front of something | *behind the library; in front of the building* |
| *beside/next to* | At the side of something | *beside the printer; next to the café* |
| *between* | In the space separating two people or things | *between the library and the café* |
| *among* | In a group or surrounded by several things | *among the students; among the trees* |
| *near/close to* | A short distance away | *near the station; close to campus* |

Use *at* to treat a place as a point or activity, *in* to emphasize being inside or within an area, and *on* for a surface or certain routes and transport. The best choice may depend on how a speaker views the location:

> She is **at the library**. (The library is the place where she is.)
>
> She is **in the library**. (She is inside the building.)

## 7.4 Prepositions of movement and direction

Prepositions of movement show where someone or something starts, travels, or arrives.

- **to** a destination: *walk to the station; send the file to a colleague*;
- **into** the inside of a place: *go into the office*;
- **onto** a surface: *place the folder onto the desk*;
- **from** a starting point: *travel from the airport*;
- **through** an interior or passage: *walk through the lobby*;
- **across** from one side to another: *walk across the courtyard*;
- **along** a route or line: *cycle along the river*;
- **past** a point: *go past the information desk*;
- **toward** a direction or destination: *head toward the east entrance*;
- **out of/off** movement away from an interior or surface: *step out of the elevator; take the book off the shelf*.

Compare *in* and *into*, and *on* and *onto*:

> The keys are **in** the drawer. (Location.)
>
> Put the keys **into** the drawer. (Movement to the inside.)
>
> The folder is **on** the desk. (Location.)
>
> Place the folder **onto** the desk. (Movement to the surface.)

In everyday English, *in* and *on* can also describe movement when the context is clear: *Put the keys in the drawer; set the folder on the desk.*

## 7.5 Prepositions for other relationships

Prepositions express more than place and time. Common examples include:

- **with** (company, tool, or characteristic): *with a colleague; write with a pen; a person with experience*;
- **by** (agent, method, or deadline): *written by the team; travel by train; finish by Friday*;
- **for** (benefit, purpose, or duration): *a message for you; a room for study; for two weeks*;
- **from** (source or starting point): *a call from the clinic; from 9:00 to 5:00*;
- **about** (topic): *a discussion about housing*;
- **of** (part, connection, or amount): *the cover of the report; a cup of tea*;
- **like** (similarity): *a response like yours*;
- **without** (absence): *without a password*;
- **as** (role or function): *work as an assistant*.

One preposition can express different relationships. For example, *by* indicates the agent in *a report by the analyst*, the method in *sent by email*, and a deadline in *submit by noon*. Read the phrase in context.

## 7.6 Prepositions and word partnerships

Many preposition choices are part of established word partnerships, sometimes called **collocations**. A collocation is a combination of words that speakers commonly use together. The choice may not follow a simple logical rule, so it is helpful to learn the whole phrase in context.

| Common expression | Example |
|---|---|
| interested **in** | She is interested **in** public health. |
| responsible **for** | He is responsible **for** the schedule. |
| good **at** | They are good **at** solving problems. |
| apply **for** a position / apply **to** an organization | I applied **for** a position **at** the clinic. |
| depend **on** | The delivery time depends **on** the weather. |
| listen **to** | Please listen **to** the announcement. |
| wait **for** | We are waiting **for** the instructor. |
| arrive **at** a specific place / arrive **in** a city or country | The bus arrives **at** the campus; we arrived **in** Seoul. |
| different **from** | This version is different **from** the original. |

When you learn a new adjective or verb, record it with its preposition and an example: *interested in a topic; depend on a result*. This is more reliable than translating the preposition from another language word for word.

## 7.7 What prepositional phrases do

Prepositional phrases add useful details. They can modify a verb, an adjective, or a noun.

### Adding information to a verb

> The students met **after class**. (When did they meet?)
>
> We waited **near the entrance**. (Where did we wait?)
>
> She opened the file **with a password**. (How or with what did she open it?)

### Adding information to a noun

> The report **on student housing** is ready.
>
> The employee **from the evening shift** left a note.

The phrases identify or give more information about *report* and *employee*.

### Adding information to an adjective

> I am **worried about the deadline**.
>
> They are **ready for the interview**.

The phrases complete or extend the meanings of *worried* and *ready*.

If a sentence has many prepositional phrases, make sure readers can tell what each phrase modifies. Too many phrases can hide the main action:

> Crowded: The report **from the team in the office near the station about the changes to the schedule for next week** is ready.
>
> Clearer: The team **in the office near the station** prepared a report **about next week's schedule changes**.

## 7.8 Common learner challenges

### Confusing *in, on,* and *at*

For time, remember the broad pattern: *at* a time, *on* a day or date, *in* a month, year, or longer period. For place, use *at* for a point or activity, *in* for inside or within an area, and *on* for a surface or certain transport. Learn common exceptions as complete expressions.

### Adding an unnecessary preposition

Some verbs take a direct object with no preposition:

> Correct: We **discussed the proposal**. (Not *discussed about the proposal*.)
>
> Correct: She **entered the room**. (Not *entered into the room* in this ordinary meaning.)

Other verbs require a preposition: *listen to the recording; wait for the bus*. Learn the whole pattern.

### Omitting a required preposition

Some adjectives and verbs are commonly followed by a preposition:

> Correct: I am interested **in** the program. (Not *interested the program*.)
>
> Correct: We depend **on** the schedule. (Not *depend the schedule*.)

### Choosing a preposition by direct translation

A phrase that uses one preposition in one language may use another in English. Learn expressions such as *afraid of, good at, arrive in,* and *wait for* as units, with examples.

### Assuming a sentence cannot end in a preposition

English often places a preposition at the end of a question or relative clause, especially in conversation. This is standard and natural:

> Who are you speaking **to**?
>
> This is the office I applied **to**.

In a very formal context, you can sometimes move the preposition earlier (*To whom are you speaking?*), but do not make a sentence awkward just to avoid a final preposition.

## 7.10 Answer key

2. **at** Central Station **at** 8:30 — Both identify a specific location and time.

4. **in** Room 204 from 9:00 **to/until** 10:15 — *In* gives the room location; *to* or *until* gives the endpoint.
5. **at** noon **at** the Garden Café — *At* is used with noon and with the café as a meeting or activity location. *In the Garden Café* is also possible if the focus is being inside it.

7. **across from** the Main Library — This is the relationship shown on the map.
8. **inside** the Science Building — *Inside* describes the lab's location. *In* is also natural.
9. **from** the East Gate **to** Room 204 — *From* marks the starting point and *to* the destination.

- **Before the morning seminar** — *before* + *the morning seminar*; tells when *stopped* happened.
- **at the café** — *at* + *the café*; tells where the writer stopped.
- **near the east gate** — *near* + *the east gate*; describes the café's location.
- **of tea** — *of* + *tea*; completes the noun phrase *a cup of tea* by specifying its contents.
- **to the library** — *to* + *the library*; tells where the writer carried the cup.

- **During the break** — *during* + *the break*; tells when the classmate asked.
- **from the design program** — *from* + *the design program*; identifies or describes the classmate.
- **about the project** — *about* + *the project*; tells the topic of the question.

## Chapter summary

Prepositions show relationships such as time, place, direction, and connection. A prepositional phrase begins with a preposition and includes its object and any related modifiers. Phrases can add information to verbs, nouns, and adjectives. Use *at, on,* and *in* according to the kind of time or place being described, while learning common exceptions and word partnerships such as *interested in* and *wait for*. Some verbs take a direct object without a preposition; others require one. English also commonly ends questions and clauses with prepositions. To choose accurately, consider the relationship, learn frequent combinations in context, and check whether the phrase makes the intended meaning clear.
