# Chapter 16. The Paragraph

## What you will learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- explain how a paragraph develops one main point or purpose;
- use topic sentences, support, and transitions when they help readers;
- build unity, coherence, and sufficient development;
- choose a pattern for organizing information;
- decide where a paragraph should begin and end; and
- plan, draft, revise, and edit a paragraph for an academic or professional audience.

## 16.1 What a paragraph does

A **paragraph** is a group of related sentences that develops one main point or purpose. In a short message, a paragraph may contain only one or two sentences. In a longer explanation, it may contain several. Its length depends on what readers need and how much the point requires.

Paragraphs help readers see how information is grouped. A new paragraph tells readers that the writer is beginning a new step, reason, example, topic, or part of the discussion. The paragraph is therefore a unit of meaning, not simply a block of a certain number of lines.

### A familiar pattern

Many paragraphs include some or all of these elements:

- A **topic sentence** states or signals the main point.
- **Supporting details** explain the point or give reasons for it.
- **Examples or evidence** make the support specific and credible.
- A **concluding or linking sentence** draws a brief conclusion or connects the paragraph to what follows.

Here is one example:

> **The campus library's extended evening hours give working students more time to study.** Many students attend classes during the day and work in the afternoon or evening. When the library stays open until midnight during exam periods, they can use quiet study areas after work. They can also meet classmates to review course material. **These later hours make campus study resources easier for working students to use.**

The first sentence introduces the main point. The next three sentences explain it with reasons and examples. The last sentence restates the value of the change. This pattern is useful, but it is not a formula every paragraph must follow. A narrative paragraph may begin with an event; a short professional update may put the result first and omit a concluding sentence. Let the purpose determine the shape.

## 16.2 Three qualities of an effective paragraph

### Unity

**Unity** means that every sentence serves the paragraph's main point or purpose. A sentence that introduces a new, unrelated topic can distract readers. Move it to another paragraph, connect it clearly to the point, or remove it.

For example, a paragraph about extended library hours should focus on student access, study time, or a closely related issue. A sentence about the cafeteria's new menu probably belongs elsewhere.

### Coherence

**Coherence** means that readers can follow how the ideas connect. A coherent paragraph presents information in a sensible order and makes relationships clear. Writers create coherence through:

- a logical sequence of ideas;
- repeated key terms or carefully chosen pronouns;
- transitions that accurately signal relationships; and
- sentence structures that guide the reader from known information to new information.

Coherence does not mean that every sentence needs a transition at the beginning. Repeating *however* or *therefore* without a real contrast or conclusion makes a paragraph less clear, not more.

### Development

**Development** means giving the main point enough explanation, detail, examples, evidence, or reasoning for the reader's purpose. A point that is obvious to the writer may not be clear to a reader who lacks the same background.

Ask: What would a reader reasonably ask after reading this point? Add the information that answers those questions. A brief status update may need only a result and next step. An academic claim may need evidence and an explanation of how that evidence supports the claim.

## 16.3 Organizing support

Choose an order that fits the relationship among the details. A paragraph may use more than one pattern, but one pattern should guide its main development.

### Example or illustration

Use examples to explain a general point or show what it looks like in practice.

> Clear instructions reduce delays during onboarding. For example, a new employee can complete payroll forms before the first day if the instructions identify the required documents and submission deadline.

### Sequence

Use time order or steps when readers need to follow a process or event.

> First, sign in to the student portal. Next, open the **Registration** page and select your courses. Review the schedule for conflicts before submitting your choices.

For procedures, number the steps when the order is essential or when readers need to refer back to a step.

### Comparison

Use comparison to explain similarities, differences, or the significance of a contrast. Make the basis of comparison clear.

> The morning workshop is designed for beginners, while the afternoon session assumes that participants have used the software before. Both sessions include guided practice, but only the afternoon session covers advanced reporting tools.

### Cause and effect

Use this pattern to explain why something happened, what resulted, or how several factors are related. Avoid claiming that one event caused another when the evidence shows only that they occurred together.

> Because the delivery arrived two days late, the team moved the product demonstration to Friday. The change gave staff time to test the equipment before the client meeting.

### Problem and solution

Describe a problem, explain why it matters, and present a response or possible solution.

> Customers often submit incomplete support forms, which delays follow-up. Adding examples beside the required fields could help customers provide the information the support team needs on the first try.

## 16.4 Transitions show relationships

A **transition** is a word, phrase, or sentence that helps readers understand the relationship between ideas. Choose one because its meaning fits the logic, not because every paragraph needs a transition word.

| Relationship | Possible signals | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Addition | also, in addition | The guide includes a checklist. It also provides sample messages. |
| Contrast | however, in contrast, although | The first plan costs less. However, it takes longer to complete. |
| Cause or result | because, therefore, as a result | The room was unavailable; as a result, we moved the meeting online. |
| Example | for example, for instance | Several tools can support revision. For example, read-aloud software can reveal missing words. |
| Sequence | first, next, finally | First, save a copy. Next, revise the draft. |
| Clarification | in other words, specifically | The deadline is firm; in other words, late submissions will not be accepted. |

Transitions can appear at the beginning, middle, or end of a sentence. Some relationships are clear without a transition because the ideas themselves connect. Read the sentences together and ask whether readers can infer the relationship. If not, add a precise signal or explain the connection directly.

## 16.5 Paragraph breaks and length

Start a new paragraph when the focus changes or when a new stage of the discussion begins. Common reasons include:

- moving to a new main point or reason;
- shifting from a problem to its solution;
- beginning a new step in a process or a new time period;
- changing speakers in dialogue; or
- separating a claim from a substantial example or explanation when that helps readers follow the argument.

There is no universal sentence count or word limit for a paragraph. A one-sentence paragraph can work for emphasis or a brief update, but frequent one-sentence paragraphs may make a longer explanation feel disconnected. A very long paragraph may need a break if it develops more than one point or becomes difficult to scan. Split it where the focus naturally changes, not merely in the middle of a connected explanation.

In professional messages, short paragraphs can make an update easier to scan. In academic writing, paragraph length depends on the complexity of the point and the evidence needed to develop it. In either setting, each paragraph should have a recognizable purpose.

## 16.6 A paragraph-writing process

Use this process for a new paragraph or a paragraph that needs substantial revision.

1. **Identify the purpose and reader.** What should the reader understand, believe, decide, or do? What does the reader already know?
2. **Gather and group ideas.** List relevant facts, reasons, examples, observations, or steps. Group related material and set aside details that do not serve the purpose.
3. **Draft a controlling point.** Write a sentence or phrase that captures what this paragraph is mainly about. It may become the topic sentence, but you can revise or move it later.
4. **Arrange the support.** Choose an order—such as example, sequence, comparison, cause and effect, or problem and solution—that makes the reasoning easy to follow.
5. **Write the paragraph.** Explain the point and include enough support. Use transitions only where they help show the relationship.
6. **Revise for meaning and flow.** Check unity, coherence, development, order, and clarity. Remove repetition and add missing explanation.
7. **Edit grammar and punctuation.** Check sentence boundaries, agreement, word forms, pronouns, punctuation, capitalization, and spelling. See Chapters 11–15 for related guidance.

You may move between steps. For instance, writing a draft may show that your controlling point is too broad or that you need another example.

## 16.7 Worked revision: from weak draft to focused paragraph

### The draft

> The student center is a useful place. It has tables and a cafe. Many students have busy schedules. The coffee line is sometimes long. The center recently extended its hours. I think this is good. Students can study there after classes, and there are group projects. The campus bus stops nearby. The center is also where clubs have events.

The draft contains relevant observations, but it does not yet develop one clear point. It moves among facilities, schedules, a long coffee line, study, transportation, and club events. Some details may be useful, but the reader cannot tell which idea controls the paragraph.

### Step 1: Choose the purpose

Suppose the writer is preparing a short academic paragraph about how extended student-center hours help students. The purpose is to explain that benefit, not to describe every feature of the building.

### Step 2: Group relevant details

Keep the information about busy schedules, later study, and group projects. The cafe, bus stop, and club events may be useful in another paragraph, but they do not directly support this point. The long coffee line is a different issue.

### Step 3: State the controlling point

> Extended student-center hours give students a flexible place to study and work together.

This point gives the paragraph a focus and suggests which details belong.

### Step 4: Arrange and develop the support

Move from the general benefit to a reason and then to a specific example. Make the relationship among the ideas explicit.

### Revised academic paragraph

> Extended student-center hours give students a flexible place to study and work together. Because many students have classes, jobs, or other responsibilities during the day, they may not be able to use campus study spaces until evening. The student center's tables provide room for individual work as well as group projects. For example, classmates can meet there after class to compare research notes or prepare a presentation. By staying open later, the center gives students more options for completing academic work on campus.

### What changed and why

- **Unity:** The revision focuses on one benefit: flexible study and group-work space.
- **Development:** It explains why later hours matter and adds a concrete example.
- **Coherence:** The ideas move from the main point, to students' schedules, to the space's uses, to a specific example, and back to the result.
- **Transitions:** *Because* signals a reason; *for example* introduces an illustration. Each transition describes a real relationship.
- **Concision:** Details about the cafe line, bus stop, and club events were removed because they did not support this paragraph's purpose.

### Workplace version

The same facts can serve a different audience and purpose. For a campus operations update, the paragraph might focus on a service change:

> The student center will stay open until 11 p.m. during the final two weeks of the term. The later schedule gives students more time to use the tables for individual study and group projects after classes and work. Facilities staff will lock the west entrance at 10 p.m.; visitors should use the main entrance after that time. We will review building use after finals and share any schedule changes by email.

This version prioritizes the schedule, practical effect, access instruction, and next step. It does not use the academic paragraph's explanatory opening because readers need an operational update.

## 16.8 Practice

### A. Develop a paragraph from notes

Use these notes to write a paragraph for a college orientation guide. Choose one main point and organize the details so a new student can follow them.

- Writing center offers free appointments
- students can bring assignment instructions and a draft
- tutors ask questions and discuss strategies
- appointments available in person or online
- book early during midterms and final projects
- tutors do not write or edit the assignment for the student

After writing, underline your controlling point. Mark the details that support it. Remove or revise any detail that does not fit the paragraph's purpose.

### B. Revise for unity and flow

Revise the paragraph so it develops one clear point. You may add a transition or explanation, move a sentence, or remove a detail.

> Our department will hold a short software training next week. The new scheduling system lets staff see room availability. Some employees prefer morning meetings. The training is on Tuesday at 2 p.m. Please bring a laptop. The cafeteria has added soup to its lunch menu. We will practice booking a room and changing a reservation. Contact Priya if you need the online meeting link.

Then answer: Who is the reader? What is the paragraph's purpose? Which sentence did you move or remove, and why?

### C. Write a short explanation or professional update

Choose one task:

1. **Explanation:** Explain one strategy that helps you prepare for an exam or complete a work task. Include a reason and a specific example.
2. **Professional update:** Write a paragraph telling a team what has been completed, what remains, and what should happen next.

Before drafting, note the reader and purpose. After drafting, check the paragraph for unity, coherence, and sufficient development.

## 16.9 Sample responses and revision guidance

### A. Sample orientation paragraph

> The campus writing center helps students strengthen their own writing through free appointments. You can bring the assignment instructions, a draft, or questions about getting started. A tutor will ask about your goals and discuss strategies you can use as you revise; tutors do not write or edit the assignment for you. Appointments are available in person and online, but times fill quickly during midterms and final projects, so book early.

This version focuses on what the writing center offers and how students can use it. The sentence about what tutors do not do clarifies expectations. The final clause gives a practical next step.

### B. Sample department update

> Our department will hold a short training on the new scheduling system next Tuesday at 2 p.m. Please bring a laptop so you can practice checking room availability, booking a room, and changing a reservation. Contact Priya if you need the online meeting link.

The cafeteria sentence was removed because it was unrelated. The training date, preparation, practice activities, and contact instruction are arranged in the order readers need them.

### C. Sample professional update

> We have completed the first review of the client survey and grouped the responses by topic. We still need to verify three entries that do not include a date. Please confirm those records by Thursday so we can prepare the summary for Friday's team meeting.

The paragraph uses a clear sequence: completed work, remaining work, and requested next action. The deadline and purpose explain why the action matters.

## Chapter summary

A paragraph develops one main point or purpose. Topic sentences, supporting details, examples, and concluding or linking sentences are useful tools, but paragraphs do not all need the same visible pattern. Effective paragraphs have unity, coherence, and enough development for their readers. Organize details by a pattern that fits their relationship, and use transitions to signal real logic. Start a new paragraph when the focus changes. Plan for the reader and purpose, group ideas, arrange support, draft, revise for meaning and flow, and then edit grammar and punctuation.
