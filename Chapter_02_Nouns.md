# Chapter 2. Nouns

## What you will learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- recognize nouns as names for people, places, things, ideas, and experiences;
- distinguish common and proper nouns, concrete and abstract nouns, and count and noncount nouns;
- form many regular plurals and recognize common irregular forms;
- use quantity words with count and noncount nouns;
- form possessives with apostrophes; and
- identify nouns functioning as subjects, objects, and complements.

## 2.1 What is a noun?

A **noun** is a word that names a person, place, thing, idea, or experience.

- People: *student, supervisor, doctor, neighbor*
- Places: *library, office, Singapore, classroom*
- Things: *laptop, key, document, coffee*
- Ideas: *patience, equality, knowledge, responsibility*
- Experiences and activities: *meeting, travel, research, conversation*

Nouns are often the main words in **noun phrases**—groups of words organized around a noun. In *the new student from Canada*, for example, *student* is the main noun. The words *the, new,* and *from Canada* give more information about it. You do not need to label every word in the phrase as a noun; focus on the word that names the person, place, thing, or idea.

## 2.2 Kinds of nouns

### Common and proper nouns

A **common noun** names a general person, place, thing, or idea: *professor, city, company, language*. A **proper noun** gives the specific name of a person, place, organization, language, or other named entity: *Professor Chen, Manila, Northstar Design, English*.

Capitalize proper nouns. Do not capitalize a common noun just because it seems important:

> The **university** opened in 1965.
>
> **East Harbor University** opened in 1965.

In the first sentence, *university* is a general reference. In the second, it is part of the institution's name. Capitalization can also depend on context: *the president* is usually lowercase when it is a general title, while *President Rivera* is capitalized when used as a title before a name. When in doubt, check the person's or organization’s official spelling and the style rules used for your document.

### Concrete and abstract nouns

A **concrete noun** names something that can be experienced through the senses: *screen, rain, music, perfume*. An **abstract noun** names an idea, quality, state, or experience: *honesty, pressure, joy, progress*.

The distinction is about the kind of thing named, not whether the noun can be counted. *Book* is concrete and countable; *equipment* is concrete but usually noncount. *Idea* is abstract and countable; *advice* is abstract and usually noncount.

### Count and noncount nouns

A **count noun** names something that can be counted as separate units. It has singular and plural forms, and a singular count noun normally needs a determiner such as *a, the, this,* or *my*:

> **a question** / **three questions**
>
> **the chair** / **the chairs**

A **noncount noun** names a substance, category, or general idea that English does not usually count as separate items. Noncount nouns generally do not take *a/an* and do not have a plural form in their usual meaning:

> **advice**, not *an advice* or *advices*
>
> **information**, not *an information* or *informations*

Common noncount nouns include *advice, information, equipment, furniture, homework, research, luggage, traffic,* and *feedback*. Some nouns that look plural are also treated as singular noncount nouns: *The news is useful.*

To count a noncount noun, use a unit or container expression:

- a **piece of advice** / two **pieces of advice**
- an **item of information** / several **items of information**
- a **piece of equipment** / three **pieces of equipment**
- a **glass of water** / two **glasses of water**

Some nouns can be count or noncount depending on their meaning. Compare:

> I drink **coffee** before class. (coffee as a substance; noncount)
>
> We ordered two **coffees**. (two cups or servings; count)

> She has **experience** in customer service. (general knowledge gained over time; noncount)
>
> She described several **experiences** from her internship. (separate events; count)

The same pattern appears with *paper* (material) and *a paper* (an essay or document), or *chicken* (food) and *a chicken* (an animal). Use the meaning in context to decide.

## 2.3 Singular and plural nouns

A **singular noun** refers to one person or thing; a **plural noun** refers to more than one. Many plural nouns follow regular spelling patterns, but some are irregular and must be learned individually.

### Regular plural spelling patterns

| Singular ending | Usual plural pattern | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Most nouns | Add *-s* | *book/books, email/emails, student/students* |
| *-s, -ss, -sh, -ch, -x,* or many nouns ending in *-z* | Add *-es* | *class/classes, dish/dishes, match/matches, box/boxes, quiz/quizzes* |
| Consonant + *y* | Change *y* to *i* and add *-es* | *city/cities, policy/policies* |
| Vowel + *y* | Add *-s* | *day/days, key/keys, survey/surveys* |
| Many nouns ending in *-f* or *-fe* | Change to *-ves* | *leaf/leaves, life/lives* |

The *-f/-fe* pattern has important exceptions: *roof/roofs, belief/beliefs,* and *chef/chefs*. Some words have more than one accepted plural or follow a pattern that varies; check a reliable dictionary when a form is unfamiliar.

Nouns ending in *-o* do not follow one dependable rule. Some add *-s* (*photo/photos, piano/pianos*); others add *-es* (*tomato/tomatoes, hero/heroes*). Learn frequent words as you meet them and check less familiar ones.

### Common irregular plurals

Some common nouns change their spelling rather than adding a regular ending:

| Singular | Plural |
|---|---|
| *person* | *people* |
| *child* | *children* |
| *man* | *men* |
| *woman* | *women* |
| *foot* | *feet* |
| *tooth* | *teeth* |
| *mouse* | *mice* |
| *goose* | *geese* |

Some nouns have the same singular and plural form: *one sheep/two sheep, one series/two series, one species/many species*. Context and quantity words show whether the meaning is singular or plural.

### Plurals in compound nouns

A **compound noun** combines two or more words to name one thing or idea, as in *bus stop, credit card,* and *mother-in-law*. Usually, the main noun takes the plural ending: *bus stops, credit cards, mothers-in-law*. Compound forms vary, so check a dictionary when unsure.

## 2.4 Quantity words with nouns

Choose a quantity word that matches whether the noun is count or noncount.

| Use with count nouns | Use with noncount nouns | Use with both |
|---|---|---|
| *many, a few, few, several, each, every* | *much, a little, little* | *some, any, a lot of, enough, no* |

Examples:

> How **many questions** do you have?
>
> How **much time** do we have?
>
> We received **some feedback** and **several suggestions**.

*Few* and *little* often suggest that an amount is smaller than wanted; *a few* and *a little* mean that at least some amount exists:

> We have **few options** left. (Not many; this sounds like a problem.)
>
> We have **a few options** left. (Some options remain.)

> There is **little time** to revise. (Almost no time.)
>
> There is **a little time** to revise. (Some time remains.)

For a noncount noun, use *much* in questions and negative statements in many contexts, but *a lot of* often sounds more natural in positive everyday statements: *We have a lot of work today.* For count nouns, use *many*: *We have many tasks today.*

## 2.5 Possessive nouns

A **possessive noun** shows ownership or another close relationship. In writing, apostrophes help mark possession.

- Singular noun: add *'s* — *the student's notebook, the manager's office*
- Plural noun ending in *-s*: add an apostrophe — *the students' notebooks, the managers' offices*
- Irregular plural not ending in *-s*: add *'s* — *the children's room, the people's choice*
- Singular noun ending in *-s*: many style guides use *'s* — *James's report*. Some house styles handle certain names differently, so be consistent with the style required for your work.

Compare the apostrophe's job in these examples:

> The **student's** question was clear. (One student; the question belongs to or is associated with that student.)
>
> The **students'** questions were clear. (More than one student; the questions belong to or are associated with them.)
>
> The **students** asked a question. (Plural noun; no possession, so no apostrophe.)

Possession can describe more than ownership. In *today's meeting*, the noun shows a time relationship. In *the company's policy*, it shows association. A later punctuation chapter reviews apostrophes, contractions, and related punctuation in more detail.

### Do not confuse possessives and contractions

Possessive nouns use apostrophes, but ordinary plurals do not. Also distinguish possessive pronouns from contractions:

- *its* = belonging to it; *it's* = *it is* or *it has*
- *whose* = belonging to whom; *who's* = *who is* or *who has*

> **The company changed its policy.** (*Its* shows association.)
>
> **It's changing the policy.** (*It's* means *it is*.)

## 2.6 Nouns in basic sentence roles

Nouns can perform different jobs in a sentence. Knowing the job helps you understand sentence structure, but it does not change the noun's class: *student* remains a noun whether it is the subject or object.

### Subject

The **subject** tells who or what the sentence is about, or who or what performs the action in a basic action sentence.

> **The consultant** arrived early.
>
> **Three reports** are missing.

The subjects are *consultant* and *reports*. The complete subject can include other words, as in *The new consultant from Taipei arrived early*; the central noun is still *consultant*.

### Object

An **object** receives an action or completes the meaning of some verbs. In the first sentence below, *the schedule* is the direct object; in the second, *the team* is the indirect object and *a reminder* is the direct object.

> I updated **the schedule**.
>
> The supervisor sent **the team** **a reminder**.

Nouns can also be objects of prepositions:

> The keys are **on the desk**.

Here, *desk* is the object of the preposition *on*.

### Complement

A **complement** completes the meaning of another part of the sentence. A noun can follow a linking verb and identify or rename the subject. This is a **subject complement**:

> Maria is **the team leader**.
>
> His goal became **a promotion**.

The noun phrases *the team leader* and *a promotion* identify or rename the subjects. The verb does not show an action received by those nouns; it links the subject to its description or identity.

## 2.7 Common learner challenges

### Using *a/an* with a noncount noun

In standard edited English, do not write *an advice* or *an information*. Use *some advice*, *a piece of advice*, *some information,* or *an item of information*.

### Making a noncount noun plural

Avoid *informations, homeworks,* and *equipments* when referring to the general categories. Use *information, homework,* and *equipment*, or count units: *two pieces of information; three homework assignments; several pieces of equipment*.

### Forgetting the plural ending—or adding it twice

Check whether a noun refers to one or more than one. Do not use both a plural ending and a plural quantity word that already changes the noun: *three reports*, not *three report* or *three reportses*.

### Leaving a singular count noun without a determiner

In a complete sentence, a singular count noun usually needs a determiner: *I sent **an email***, not *I sent email*. Some fixed expressions and headlines differ, but the determiner is the reliable choice for ordinary sentences.

### Capitalizing common nouns

Capitalize a specific name, not every important-sounding word. Write *the department* unless *Department* is part of an official name or a document's specific naming convention.

### Adding an apostrophe to every plural

An apostrophe does not make a noun plural. Write *students* for more than one student and *student's* for something belonging to one student. Write *students'* for something belonging to multiple students.

### C. Edit the notice

> **STUDENT CENTER NOTICE**
>
> The student center will close at 8:00 p.m. on Friday for repair. Please take your **belongings** with you. The **staff** will move three **pieces of furniture** and several **pieces of equipment** from the study room. If you **need information** about the changes, ask one of the **advisers** at the front desk. The advisers can give you **some advice** and answer your **questions**.

The nine corrections are *belongnings → belongings*; *staffs → staff* for the group of employees; *furnitures → pieces of furniture*; *equipments → pieces of equipment*; *needs → need* because the subject *you* takes *need*; *informations → information*; *advisor's → advisers* because this is a plural noun, not a possessive; *an advice → some advice* because *advice* is noncount; and *question's → questions* because the notice means more than one question, not possession. ## Chapter summary

Nouns name people, places, things, ideas, and experiences. They may be common or proper, concrete or abstract, count or noncount. Count nouns have singular and plural forms; plural spelling follows several common patterns, with irregular forms to learn. Noncount nouns such as *advice* and *information* usually do not take *a/an* or a plural ending, but can be counted with units such as *a piece of*. Possessive nouns use apostrophes to show ownership or association; ordinary plurals do not. In sentences, nouns can function as subjects, objects, and complements. To choose a noun form, check its meaning in context, whether it is countable, how many are meant, and what job it performs in the sentence.
