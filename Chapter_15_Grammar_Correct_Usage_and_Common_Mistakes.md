# Chapter 15. Grammar: Correct Usage, Rules, and Common Mistakes

## What you will learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- distinguish grammar, usage, and style;
- choose language that fits standard edited academic or professional English while recognizing valid variation;
- use a repeatable editing routine to check sentence structure, forms, agreement, and mechanics;
- correct common word-choice and sentence errors without treating every preference as an absolute rule; and
- use dictionaries, learner references, and style guides to investigate a language question.

## 15.1 Grammar, usage, and style

People often use the word *grammar* to mean any language rule, but it helps to distinguish three related ideas.

- **Grammar** is the system of patterns that shapes phrases and sentences. It includes word order, verb forms, agreement, and how clauses connect.
- **Usage** is the way words and forms are accepted or preferred in a particular community, situation, or variety of English. Usage can vary by region, profession, and level of formality.
- **Style** is a writer's choice about clarity, tone, emphasis, and consistency for a purpose and audience.

For example, the sentence *The team is ready* follows a common American English pattern that treats a collective noun as one unit. In some other English varieties, *The team are ready* is also used when the group members are emphasized. A style guide, publication, or course may ask writers to choose one form and use it consistently.

### Standard edited English and English varieties

This book focuses on **standard edited English**—the conventions commonly expected in academic and professional writing, such as college assignments, reports, applications, and formal workplace messages. That focus helps readers meet specific expectations in those settings. It does not mean English has only one valid variety. People use English in many communities, dialects, regions, and registers, each with its own patterns.

A choice may be appropriate in conversation or one variety but not fit a particular assignment or workplace document. When editing, ask:

1. What does the writer mean?
2. Who will read this, and what conventions does the situation expect?
3. Is the wording clear and consistent for that audience?
4. Is the issue a grammar pattern, a context-dependent usage choice, or a style preference?

This approach lets you adapt writing without treating differences in English as evidence that a speaker or community is deficient.

## 15.2 A core editing checklist

When you revise, check the meaning and sentence structure before polishing smaller details.

### Verb tense and form

Check that verb forms match the timeline and the structure:

> Yesterday, we **submitted** the form. (Completed past event.)
>
> We **have submitted** the form. (Past action connected to the present.)
>
> She **has written** the summary. (Past participle after *has*.)

Watch for irregular forms, missing helping verbs, and unnecessary tense shifts. See Chapter 5 for verb forms and tense/aspect.

### Agreement

Find the subject before choosing the verb. Nouns inside an intervening phrase do not control it:

> The list of required documents **is** attached. (*List* is the subject.)

Check that pronouns have clear antecedents and that their forms fit their roles. See Chapter 11 for subject-verb and pronoun-antecedent agreement.

### Pronoun case and reference

Use subject forms for subjects and object forms for objects or objects of prepositions:

> **Mina and I** reviewed the draft. / The editor contacted **Mina and me**.

Make sure readers can tell what *it, they,* or *this* refers to. Repeat a noun when that is clearer than using a vague pronoun. Chapter 3 covers pronouns in detail.

### Articles and determiners

Check whether a singular count noun needs a determiner, whether *a/an/the* fits the context, and whether a noncount noun is being treated correctly:

> She sent **an email** about **the schedule**.
>
> We need **some information**, not *an information*.

See Chapters 2 and 9 for countability and articles.

### Prepositions

Check that the preposition is part of a natural, established phrase and that it is needed:

> interested **in** the program; wait **for** the bus; discuss the proposal (no *about* needed after *discuss* in this meaning).

Learn common combinations with examples rather than translating each preposition word for word. See Chapter 7.

### Modifiers

Place modifiers near the word they describe. Check that an opening phrase names the person or thing doing the action:

> Unclear: After reviewing the report, **the deadline** was extended.
>
> Clear: After reviewing the report, **the manager** extended the deadline.

### Sentence boundaries

Check that each formal sentence has a complete independent clause. Repair fragments, fused run-ons, and comma splices. See Chapters 12–14 for clauses, sentence boundaries, and punctuation.

## 15.3 Commonly confused words and forms

Words that sound alike or look similar can be difficult even for fluent writers. Identify the meaning needed in the sentence, then choose the matching form.

### *There, their,* and *they're*

- **there** refers to a place or introduces an expression: *Leave the package **there**; **there is** a note on the desk.*
- **their** is possessive: *The students submitted **their** forms.*
- **they're** means *they are*: ***They're** joining the call.*

> **They're** leaving **their** files over **there**.

### *Its* and *it's*

- **its** is possessive: *The company changed **its** name.*
- **it's** means *it is* or *it has*: ***It's** ready; **it's** been reviewed.*

The apostrophe in *it's* marks a contraction, not possession.

### *Your* and *you're*

- **your** is possessive: *Is this **your** notebook?*
- **you're** means *you are*: ***You're** welcome to join us.*

### *Then* and *than*

- **then** refers to time, order, or a result: *First we reviewed the notes; **then** we began the exercise.*
- **than** introduces a comparison: *This version is clearer **than** the first.*

### *Fewer* and *less*

As a general guide, use **fewer** with plural count nouns and **less** with noncount amounts:

> **fewer meetings** / **less time**
>
> **fewer errors** / **less confusion**

Usage is not always that simple. English commonly uses *less* for a total amount, measurement, or time (*less than ten dollars; less than an hour; less than five miles*). Product labels, idioms, and fixed expressions may also use *less* with a plural noun. For clear academic or professional prose, use *fewer* for separately countable items and *less* for a continuous amount unless a familiar expression calls for another form.

### *Who* and *whom*

**Who** is the subject form; **whom** is the object form. In a question, try substituting *he/she* or *him/her*:

> **Who** called? (**She** called.)
>
> **Whom** did you call? (You called **him**.)

In everyday speech, many speakers use *who* in both questions. In formal writing, *whom* is common after a preposition (*to whom, with whom*) and when it is clearly an object. If *whom* makes a sentence awkward, rewrite it: *Who should I send the form to?* For more, see Chapter 3.

### *Affect* and *effect*

- **affect** is usually a verb meaning “to influence”: *The delay may **affect** delivery.*
- **effect** is usually a noun meaning “result”: *The change had a positive **effect**.*

There are less common uses of each as another part of speech, but the verb/noun distinction covers many everyday cases.

### *Accept* and *except*

- **accept** means “to receive or agree to”: *She **accepted** the invitation.*
- **except** means “not including”: *Everyone attended **except** Omar.*

### *Advice* and *advise*

- **advice** is a noun: *Thank you for your **advice**.* It is usually noncount.
- **advise** is a verb: *I **advise** you to save a copy.*

### *Principal* and *principle*

- **principal** can mean a school leader or “main”: *the **principal** reason; the school **principal**.*
- **principle** is a rule or belief: *a basic **principle** of design.*

### *Complement* and *compliment*

- **complement** means to complete or go well with: *The blue headings **complement** the chart.*
- **compliment** means praise: *The client gave the team a **compliment**.*

### *Lie* and *lay*

In common standard usage:

- **lie** means to recline or be in a position; it does not take a direct object: *I **lie** down; yesterday I **lay** down; I have **lain** down.*
- **lay** means to put something down and takes an object: *I **lay** the folder on the desk; yesterday I **laid** it there; I have **laid** it there.*

The past tense of *lie* (*lay*) looks like the base form of *lay*, which makes this pair tricky. Ask whether the sentence names a person or thing reclining, or someone placing an object.

## 15.4 Sentence patterns that often need editing

### Subject-verb mismatch

Find the true subject rather than matching the verb to the nearest noun:

> Incorrect: The collection of forms **are** on the desk.
>
> Correct: The collection of forms **is** on the desk.

The subject is *collection*. See Chapter 11.

### Faulty parallelism

When a sentence connects two or more items, keep them in the same grammatical form:

> Not parallel: The role requires **planning events, to answer email, and meeting clients**.
>
> Parallel: The role requires **planning events, answering email, and meeting clients**.

Parallel structure helps readers compare the items.

### Double negatives in standard edited contexts

In many varieties of English, **negative concord**—using more than one negative word to express one negative meaning—is a grammatical pattern. In standard edited academic or professional English, however, readers may expect one negative marker for one negative meaning:

> Informal or dialectal pattern: I **didn't see nobody**.
>
> Standard edited form: I **didn't see anybody**. / I **saw nobody**.

The point is to meet the conventions of the setting, not to label a dialect or speaker as illogical. Also, two negative words can sometimes have a deliberate positive or contrasting meaning (*not uncommon* means “fairly common”), so interpret the intended meaning before editing.

### Missing or unnecessary prepositions

Some expressions require a preposition, and others do not:

> Correct: We **discussed the schedule**. (Not *discussed about the schedule* in this meaning.)
>
> Correct: We **talked about the schedule**.

Check the verb's pattern and meaning.

### Unclear or misplaced modifiers

A modifier should sit near the word it describes:

> Unclear: The student submitted the form **to the adviser with the missing page**.

Who has the missing page—the student, the adviser, or the form? Name the intended relationship:

> Clear: The student submitted **the form, which was missing a page,** to the adviser.

### Overly passive or indirect wording

Passive voice can be useful when the action or result matters more than the doer. If the reader needs to know who acted, name that person:

> Less direct: The address was entered incorrectly.
>
> Clearer when responsibility matters: **The clerk entered the address incorrectly.**

Similarly, replace inflated phrases with direct wording when the meaning stays the same:

> Wordy: We **made a decision to postpone** the meeting.
>
> Concise: We **decided to postpone** the meeting.

## 15.5 Corrections depend on context

An editor should be able to explain why a change helps in a particular context. A correction may be needed because it:

- makes the sentence grammatical under the expected standard;
- prevents an unclear or unintended meaning;
- matches the intended level of formality;
- follows a required style guide; or
- makes the writing more concise or consistent.

Not every difference is an error. Some choices are regional or depend on whether the writer emphasizes a group as one unit or as individuals. Some editors prefer one wording even though another is also grammatical. Label preferences honestly:

- **Grammar issue:** *Does she works here?* should be *Does she work here?* because *does* carries the present-tense marking and is followed by the base verb.
- **Usage choice:** *Who did you speak to?* is common in conversation; *To whom did you speak?* is more formal.
- **Style preference:** *We met at 9 a.m.* is more concise than *We had a meeting at the time of 9 a.m.*

Explain the context and the reason for a correction rather than treating every house-style preference as a universal rule.

## 15.6 A repeatable editing routine

Use the same sequence each time so that you do not focus on commas while the main idea is still unclear.

1. **Read for meaning.** What is the main point? Does the message say what you intend?
2. **Check sentence structure.** Find subjects, verbs, clauses, and sentence boundaries. Repair fragments, run-ons, and confusing structures.
3. **Check word forms and agreement.** Review verb tense and form, subject-verb agreement, pronoun case and reference, articles, prepositions, and modifiers.
4. **Review punctuation, capitalization, and spelling.** Apply the conventions appropriate for the audience and style guide.
5. **Read aloud or use another method.** Reading aloud can reveal missing words and awkward transitions. A text-to-speech tool, printed copy, or fresh review after a break can help you notice what your eyes skipped.
6. **Make a final consistency pass.** Check names, dates, key terms, formatting, and repeated choices.

For long documents, edit in more than one pass. Trying to check meaning, grammar, punctuation, and formatting all at once makes it easier to overlook a problem.

## 15.7 Use language tools thoughtfully

Different references answer different questions:

- A **dictionary** gives meanings, spelling, pronunciation, word class, and usage labels.
- A **learner's dictionary** adds clear definitions, example sentences, common word partnerships, and grammar patterns for learners.
- A **grammar reference** explains sentence structures and forms such as clauses, agreement, and verb patterns.
- A **style guide** sets consistent editorial choices for a publication, course, or organization.
- A **spell-checker or grammar checker** can point to a possible problem, but it may misread names, dialect, technical terms, or a sentence's intended meaning.

### A five-step way to check a question

1. **State the exact question.** For example: “Should I write *fewer time* or *less time* here?”
2. **Read the whole sentence.** Identify the intended meaning and the word's job.
3. **Check a reliable reference.** Look for the relevant meaning, usage label, and example—not just the first suggested correction.
4. **Compare examples.** See how the word works in a similar sentence and whether regional or formal usage is noted.
5. **Apply the answer and reread.** Confirm that the correction preserves the intended meaning and fits the document's style.

If a checker proposes a change, ask what rule or clarity problem it has detected. Keep the change if it helps; reject or adapt it if it changes the meaning, ignores the context, or conflicts with the required style.

## 15.9 Answer key

### A. Proofread a student email

One possible revision:

> **Subject: Question About My Assignment**
>
> Hi Professor Nguyen,
>
> I am writing to ask whether you received my assignment. I submitted it on Monday, but the system says it has not received the file. Could you please advise me on what I should do? I have attached a copy, and I would be grateful if you could confirm receipt.
>
> Thanks,
> Jordan

Corrections and choices:

- *subject* and names are capitalized in the heading and greeting; *Monday, Professor Nguyen,* and *Jordan* are proper names or titles before names.
- *recieved* is corrected to **received** (spelling).
- *monday* is capitalized as **Monday**.
- *the system say* becomes **the system says** (singular subject-verb agreement).
- *hasn't recieved it* becomes **has not received the file**. The correct spelling is *received*; naming *the file* clarifies what the system did not receive. The contraction is expanded for a more formal tone.
- *advice me* becomes **advise me** because *advise* is the verb; *advice* is a noun. **Advise me on what I should do** is a natural pattern.
- The question takes a question mark.
- **Could you** is more consistent with the polite hypothetical request than *can* after *I would be grateful*. This is a tone and consistency choice, not the same kind of error as misspelling *received*.
- **confirm receipt** is concise and specific; *confirm* alone is also possible if the context is clear.

### B. Proofread a short work report

One possible revision:

> The number of customer **requests has** increased this month. Each of the service teams **was** asked to record **its** response time, and the data **were** reviewed on Friday. **Fewer** customers were waiting after the schedule change. The report recommends **adding** two evening shifts, **improving** the online form, and **asking** managers to review the queue every morning. The manager said the change had a positive **effect** on service. **However,** the report does not include all of the teams' results.

Corrections and reasons:

- Change *request* to plural **requests** because it refers to multiple customer requests. The main subject is **number**, which is singular, so the verb is **has**.
- **Each** is singular, so **was** agrees with it. *Its* refers to each team as a unit; *their* is also used by some writers with singular antecedents, but *its* maintains grammatical singular agreement here.
- The comma splice between *time* and *the data* is repaired by adding *and* and making the relationship clear.
- **Data** is used as a plural noun in this report, so **were** agrees. In some fields and contexts, *data* is treated as a mass noun with singular agreement; choose a convention and use it consistently.
- **Fewer customers** follows the count-noun pattern; *customers* are countable people. **Were waiting** gives a clear past-time report.
- **Adding, improving, and asking** are parallel forms. The original mixed an infinitive, an *-ing* form, and a clause.
- **Effect** is a noun meaning “result”; *affect* is usually the verb meaning “influence.”
- A period repairs the comma splice before *however*. **However,** begins a new sentence and takes a comma.
- **Teams' results** is plural possessive: the results belong to multiple teams.

The report uses standard edited forms and a more direct structure. The writer could use *less* in a different expression of amount, but **fewer customers** is the clearest choice here.

### C. Proofread a sample online post

One possible revision that keeps an informal voice:

> You're going to love this place! It's not far from the station, and there are fewer people in the morning. I could have stayed longer, but it was too late. They're doing open-mic nights every Friday, so bring a friend!

Corrections:

- **You're** means *you are*; **your** is possessive.
- **It's** means *it is*; **its** is possessive.
- **from the station** is the natural preposition for distance in this sentence; *then* refers to time or sequence, while **than** is used for comparisons.
- **there are fewer people** uses *there* to introduce the clause and *fewer* with the count noun *people*.
- **could have** is the standard written form; *could of* results from the sound of the contraction *could've*.
- **too late** means “later than suitable”; *to* is used in infinitives or as a preposition.
- **They're** means *they are*; **their** is possessive.
- **Friday** is capitalized as a day of the week. **Open-mic** is hyphenated as a compound modifier in this example; a venue's official event name may use a different form.
- The exclamation point after the first sentence preserves enthusiasm. The informal, conversational tone remains suitable for a social post.

### D. Explain the audience and context

Answers will vary. A strong response distinguishes a grammar or spelling correction from a style choice. For example, a student's email to a professor uses a greeting, complete sentences, and a polite request. A public online post can keep contractions and enthusiasm while correcting word confusion that could distract or change the intended meaning.

## Chapter summary

Grammar describes the patterns that shape sentences; usage describes choices accepted in a community or context; style describes decisions about clarity, tone, purpose, and consistency. This book teaches standard edited English for academic and professional settings while recognizing that English has many valid varieties. Effective editing checks meaning, sentence structure, verb forms, agreement, pronouns, articles, prepositions, modifiers, boundaries, punctuation, and spelling. Commonly confused words are easier to choose when you identify their meaning and grammatical job. Corrections should be explained in context rather than presented as universal judgments about every variety of English. Use dictionaries, learner references, grammar guides, and style manuals to investigate questions, and check whether a suggested change preserves your meaning and suits your audience.
