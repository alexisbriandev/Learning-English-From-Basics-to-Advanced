# Chapter 3. Pronouns

## What you will learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- explain what pronouns do and identify their antecedents;
- choose personal pronouns in subject, object, and possessive forms;
- use reflexive, intensive, demonstrative, interrogative, and indefinite pronouns;
- select pronoun case according to a pronoun's job in a sentence;
- use singular *they* respectfully and clearly; and
- revise pronouns whose references are vague or confusing.

## 3.1 What is a pronoun?

A **pronoun** is a word that stands in for a noun or noun phrase. The noun or noun phrase a pronoun refers to is its **antecedent**.

> Lena borrowed the laptop because **she** needed it for class.

In this sentence, *she* refers to *Lena*, and *it* refers to *the laptop*. These pronouns help the writer avoid repeating the nouns. A pronoun works well when readers can tell what it refers to.

Sometimes a pronoun refers to an idea or a whole statement rather than one noun:

> The train was delayed again. **This** frustrated the passengers.

Here *this* refers to the train being delayed again. This kind of reference can be useful, but the writer should make the connection clear.

## 3.2 Personal pronouns

**Personal pronouns** refer to people or things and change form according to their role. The main forms are subject, object, and possessive.

| Person | Subject | Object | Possessive determiner | Possessive pronoun |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First person singular | *I* | *me* | *my* | *mine* |
| Second person singular or plural | *you* | *you* | *your* | *yours* |
| Third person singular | *he, she, it* | *him, her, it* | *his, her, its* | *his, hers* |
| First person plural | *we* | *us* | *our* | *ours* |
| Third person plural | *they* | *them* | *their* | *theirs* |

### Subject forms

Use a **subject pronoun** for the person or thing doing the action or being described as the subject.

> **I** take notes during the lecture.
>
> **She** works in the student services office.
>
> **They** are ready for the presentation.

### Object forms

Use an **object pronoun** for the person or thing receiving an action or following a preposition.

> The instructor called **me**.
>
> We invited **them** to the meeting.
>
> Please send the form to **her**.

Object pronouns also follow prepositions such as *to, for, with, between,* and *from*:

> The supervisor spoke with **him**.
>
> The decision is between **you and me**.

### Possessive determiners and possessive pronouns

Possessive forms show that something belongs or is related to someone or something. They have two patterns:

- A **possessive determiner** comes before a noun: *my notebook, her question, their office*.
- A **possessive pronoun** stands in place of a noun phrase: *The notebook is mine. The question was hers. The office is theirs.*

> Is this **your** charger? The black one is **mine**.

In this book, *my, your, his, her, its, our,* and *their* are called possessive determiners when they come before nouns. Some grammar books call them possessive adjectives. Possessive pronouns include *mine, yours, his, hers, ours,* and *theirs*.

Possessive pronouns do not use apostrophes: *yours, hers,* and *theirs* are correct; *your's, her's,* and *their's* are not. Also distinguish *its* (possessive) from *it's* (*it is* or *it has*):

> The device lost **its** connection. (**Its** is possessive.)
>
> **It's** losing the connection again. (**It's** means *it is*.)

## 3.3 Pronoun case depends on the job

**Case** is the form a pronoun takes to show its grammatical role. In modern English, the main personal-pronoun distinction is between subject and object forms: *I/me, she/her, he/him,* and *they/them*.

Use subject forms for subjects and object forms for objects and objects of prepositions:

> **He** sent the schedule to **us**.

*He* is the subject of *sent*; *us* follows the preposition *to*.

### Pronouns joined to nouns or other pronouns

The same case rule applies when a pronoun is joined to another person or thing:

> **Jordan and I** prepared the slides. (Subject)
>
> The coordinator thanked **Jordan and me**. (Object)

A useful check is to temporarily remove the other name:

> Jordan and **I** prepared the slides. → **I** prepared the slides.
>
> The coordinator thanked Jordan and **me**. → The coordinator thanked **me**.

If the remaining pronoun sounds wrong, choose the other case. This quick check helps with common choices such as *my colleague and I* versus *my colleague and me*.

### *Who* and *whom*

*Who* is a subject form; *whom* is an object form. In a question, try replacing the word with *he* or *she* (subject) and *him* or *her* (object):

> **Who** sent the invitation? (**She** sent it.)
>
> **Whom** did you invite? (You invited **her**.)

In everyday conversation, many speakers use *who* instead of *whom*, especially in questions. In formal writing, *whom* is common after a preposition (*to whom, with whom*) and in carefully edited questions. If *whom* sounds unnatural or makes the sentence hard to read, rewrite it:

> To **whom** should I send the form? (Formal)
>
> Who should I send the form to? (More conversational)

### A common case error: *I* or *me*?

Writers sometimes choose *I* because it sounds more formal, even when the pronoun is an object. Decide based on the pronoun's job:

> Please contact **Ari and me** after the interview. (*Me* is the object of *contact*.)
>
> **Ari and I** will contact you. (*I* is part of the subject.)

## 3.4 Reflexive and intensive pronouns

**Reflexive pronouns** end in *-self* or *-selves*: *myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves,* and *themselves*. They refer back to the subject of the clause.

> I reminded **myself** to save the file.
>
> They introduced **themselves** before the discussion.

The subject and reflexive pronoun refer to the same person or people. Reflexive pronouns can also follow a preposition when the meaning points back to the subject:

> She kept the notes beside **herself**.

An **intensive pronoun** has the same form as a reflexive pronoun but adds emphasis; it is not required to complete the sentence.

> The director **herself** welcomed the guests.
>
> We **ourselves** checked the final figures.

Remove the intensive pronoun and the basic sentence remains complete: *The director welcomed the guests.*

Do not use a reflexive form simply to sound formal. Use the ordinary subject or object form when the pronoun does not refer back to the subject:

> Please send the draft to **me**. (Not *to myself*.)
>
> **Nora and I** will attend. (Not *Nora and myself*.)

## 3.5 Demonstrative and interrogative pronouns

### Demonstrative pronouns

The **demonstrative pronouns** *this, that, these,* and *those* point to particular people, things, or ideas.

> **This** is the form I need.
>
> **Those** were useful suggestions.

These words can also be determiners when a noun follows: *this form, those suggestions*. Compare *This is helpful* (pronoun) with *This explanation is helpful* (determiner before a noun).

When *this* or *that* refers to a whole idea, name the idea if the reference could be unclear:

> The office changed its hours without updating the website. **This change** confused visitors.

The noun *change* makes the reference more specific than *This confused visitors*.

### Interrogative pronouns

An **interrogative pronoun** introduces a question. Common forms are *who, whom, whose, what,* and *which*.

- **Who** called the clinic? (asks about a subject)
- **Whom** did the clinic call? (asks about an object; formal)
- **Whose** is this? (asks who owns it)
- **What** happened after the meeting?
- **Which** do you prefer, tea or coffee?

Some of these words can also introduce questions when followed by a noun: *Which route should we take?* In that example, *which* is a determiner; *route* is the noun.

## 3.6 Indefinite pronouns

An **indefinite pronoun** refers to a person or thing without naming exactly who or what it is. Common examples include:

- *someone, anyone, everyone, no one*
- *something, anything, everything, nothing*
- *each, either, neither*
- *all, some, many, few, several, both*

> **Someone** left a notebook in the meeting room.
>
> **Several** asked for a copy.

Some indefinite pronouns are grammatically singular, some are plural, and some depend on the noun or meaning they refer to. For example, *everyone* is grammatically singular: *Everyone has a badge.* Words such as *some* can refer to a singular noncount noun or plural count nouns: *Some of the information is missing; some of the forms are missing.* You will study pronoun agreement in Chapter 11.

## 3.7 Singular *they* and respectful reference

**Singular they** uses *they, them, their,* or *themselves/themself* for one person. It has two common uses:

1. **A specific person who uses they/them pronouns:**

   > Alex said **they** would share **their** notes after class.

   When a person has shared their pronouns, use those pronouns. In this use, singular *they* takes the same verb form as plural *they*: *Alex said they **are** ready*, not *they is ready*.

2. **A person whose gender is unknown or unspecified:**

   > If a student has a question, **they** can speak with the adviser.

   This generic use is common in contemporary English. Chicago's 17th edition recognized singular *they* for a specific person who uses it; its guidance on generic singular *they* was more cautious. Later Chicago guidance endorses generic singular *they* in some formal contexts. Because this book follows the 17th edition where applicable, it will respect a specific person's stated pronouns and use singular *they* where it is natural and clear. In highly formal work, follow the instructor's, employer's, or publisher's style guidance. [Chicago's discussion distinguishes these uses](https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/qanda/data/faq/topics/Pronouns/faq0031.html).

If readers may not know a person's pronouns, use the person's name until you learn them, ask politely when appropriate, or rephrase without guessing. For example, *Taylor submitted the report. Taylor will present it tomorrow* is clear while avoiding an assumption. Avoid switching between pronouns for the same person.

Singular *they* can also take *themself* when the meaning is clearly singular; *themselves* is also widely used. Follow the person's preference or the style required for your work. The main goals are respect and clarity.

## 3.8 Make pronoun reference clear

A pronoun should point clearly to its antecedent. If a sentence contains two possible antecedents, readers may not know which one the pronoun means.

### Vague *it*

> The report was placed beside the printer, but **it** was broken.

What was broken—the report or the printer? Name the intended noun:

> The report was placed beside the printer, but **the printer** was broken.

### Vague *they*

> The instructor spoke with the student after **they** missed class.

Did the student miss class, or did the instructor? Repeat the intended noun or restructure:

> After the student missed class, the instructor spoke with **them**.

### Vague *this* or *that*

> The team changed the schedule twice. **This** caused confusion.

Here the reference is probably clear, but a noun can make it even more precise:

> The team changed the schedule twice. **These repeated changes** caused confusion.

### Useful revision strategies

- Repeat the noun when two antecedents are possible.
- Replace a vague *this* or *that* with a specific noun phrase, such as *this delay* or *that decision*.
- Move the pronoun closer to its antecedent when doing so clarifies the connection.
- Rewrite the sentence so the person or thing is named in the clause where it acts.
- Do not remove every pronoun. Repetition is useful when it prevents confusion, but unnecessary repetition can make writing awkward.

## 3.9 Common learner challenges

### Using subject case where object case is needed

In *The adviser called Sam and I*, the pronoun is an object, so use *me*: *The adviser called Sam and me.* Remove *Sam and* to check: *The adviser called me.*

### Using *whom* without checking its role

*Whom* is not automatically the more correct choice. Use it when the pronoun is an object in formal writing; use *who* for a subject. The *he/she* versus *him/her* substitution test can help.

### Using *myself* as a formal substitute for *me*

Reflexive forms refer back to the subject or add emphasis. Write *Please contact me*, not *Please contact myself*, unless the subject and object are the same person in context (*I reminded myself*).

### Leaving an unclear pronoun reference

If *it, they,* or *this* might point to more than one thing, repeat or name the intended referent. A few extra words are better than a reader guessing.

## 3.10 Practice

### A. Replace repeated nouns

Rewrite each pair or short passage to reduce unnecessary repetition. Keep the meaning clear.

1. Mina called Mina's adviser because Mina had a question about Mina's schedule.
2. The employees received the employees' badges after the employees completed the training.
3. I printed the report, and I gave the report to the project lead before I left the office.
4. Dr. Patel met with Dr. Patel's class. Dr. Patel told the class that Dr. Patel would post the slides.

### B. Choose the correct pronoun form

1. (**She / Her**) and Daniel will lead the workshop.
2. The coordinator sent the confirmation to Priya and (**I / me**).
3. The final decision is between you and (**I / me**).
4. (**Who / Whom**) prepared the budget?
5. (**Who / Whom**) did the team select for the internship?
6. The blue folder is (**her / hers**), and the green one is (**our / ours**).
7. I (**myself / me**) checked the room before the guests arrived.
8. The guest speaker introduced (**her / herself**) to the students.
9. (**This / These**) are the updated instructions.
10. Is (**these / this**) your desk?

### C. Revise the ambiguous paragraph

Rewrite the paragraph so every pronoun has a clear reference. There are at least three places where the reader may not know what a pronoun refers to.

> Jordan gave Lee the revised presentation after they spoke with the supervisor. They said it needed a new title, but this was not what Lee expected. The supervisor told them to update it before Friday, and they agreed.

You may choose who *they* refers to, but make your choice clear and keep it consistent. More than one revision is possible.

### D. Apply it

Write a short introduction of a classmate, colleague, or fictional team member. Use at least four different pronoun types from this chapter. If writing about a real person, use that person's stated pronouns or use their name until you know which pronouns they use. Underline each pronoun and draw an arrow to its antecedent where applicable.

## 3.11 Answer key

### A. Replace repeated nouns

Answers may vary. These examples preserve clear references:

1. **Mina called her adviser because she had a question about her schedule.** *She* and *her* refer to Mina.
2. **The employees received their badges after they completed the training.** *Their* and *they* refer to the employees.
3. **I printed the report and gave it to the project lead before I left the office.** *It* refers to the report; *I* refers to the writer.
4. **Dr. Patel met with the class and announced that the instructor would post the slides.** Naming *the instructor* avoids repeating Dr. Patel and keeps the reference clear.

### B. Choose the correct pronoun form

1. **She** and Daniel — The pronoun is part of the subject. Test: *She will lead the workshop.*
2. Priya and **me** — The pronoun is the object of *to*. Test: *The coordinator sent the confirmation to me.*
3. between you and **me** — The pronoun follows the preposition *between*.
4. **Who** — It is the subject of *prepared*. Test: *She prepared the budget.*
5. **Whom** — In formal usage, it is the object of *select*. Test: *The team selected him.* In conversation, many speakers would say *Who did the team select?*
6. **hers; ours** — Both stand in for noun phrases; no noun follows.
7. **myself** — It adds emphasis to *I*; *I checked the room* remains a complete sentence.
8. **herself** — The speaker introduced the same person who is the subject.
9. **These** — It is the plural subject pronoun matching *are* and stands for the instructions.
10. **this** — It comes before *desk*, so it is a demonstrative determiner, not a pronoun. The sentence is singular.

### C. Revise the ambiguous paragraph

Answers will vary. One possible revision is:

> Jordan spoke with the supervisor about Lee's presentation. **The supervisor** said the presentation needed a new title, but **Lee** had not expected that feedback. The supervisor asked **Jordan** to update the presentation before Friday, and **Jordan** agreed.

This version names each person and replaces vague *it* and *this* with specific nouns or phrases. Another clear version could assign the conversation or feedback to Lee, as long as the writer makes that choice explicit.

### D. Apply it

Answers will vary. Check that the pronouns fit their grammatical roles and that the reader can identify each antecedent. A demonstrative or interrogative pronoun may not have a noun antecedent in the same way as a personal pronoun, so label its role rather than drawing an unclear arrow.

## Chapter summary

Pronouns stand in for nouns or noun phrases, and their antecedents are the words or ideas they refer to. Personal pronouns change form according to case: subject forms such as *I* and *they* do the action or serve as subjects; object forms such as *me* and *them* receive an action or follow a preposition. Possessive determiners come before nouns, while possessive pronouns stand alone. Reflexive pronouns point back to a subject; intensive pronouns add emphasis. Demonstrative, interrogative, and indefinite pronouns point out, ask about, or refer generally to people and things. Singular *they* is used for individuals who use it and for people whose gender is unknown or unspecified; follow a person's stated pronouns and make references clear. When a pronoun could refer to more than one person or thing, repeat the noun or revise the sentence.
