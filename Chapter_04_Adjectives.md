# Chapter 4. Adjectives

## What you will learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- recognize adjectives that describe or limit nouns and pronouns;
- identify adjectives before nouns and after linking verbs;
- arrange multiple adjectives in a natural order without treating the pattern as a fixed formula;
- form and use comparative and superlative adjectives;
- recognize participial adjectives and distinguish them from verb forms; and
- choose adjectives or adverbs in common pairs such as *good/well*.

## 4.1 What is an adjective?

An **adjective** describes or limits a noun or pronoun. It can tell what kind, which one, or what something is like.

> We moved to a **quiet** room.
>
> **Several** people joined the discussion.
>
> The route was **short**.

In these examples, *quiet* describes *room*, *several* limits which or how many *people*, and *short* describes *route*. In this book's terminology, words such as *several* and *this* that identify or limit a noun are called **determiners** (see Chapter 1). Adjectives generally describe a quality, condition, or characteristic.

Adjectives can also describe pronouns:

> The proposal was **unusual**, but it seemed **practical**.

*Unusual* and *practical* describe the proposal referred to by *it*.

## 4.2 Where adjectives appear

### Before a noun

An adjective commonly appears before the noun it describes. This position is called **attributive** use.

> a **useful** guide
>
> **fresh** coffee
>
> an **international** conference

An adjective can appear with a determiner and other modifiers:

> **The two helpful advisers** answered my questions.

Here, *the* and *two* are determiners; *helpful* is an adjective; *advisers* is the noun.

### After a linking verb

An adjective often follows a **linking verb**, which connects the subject to a description or identity rather than expressing an action directed at an object. Common linking verbs include forms of *be* and verbs such as *seem, become, feel, look, sound, smell,* and *taste* when they describe the subject.

> The instructions **are clear**.
>
> The room **seems comfortable**.
>
> This soup **tastes salty**.

The adjectives *clear, comfortable,* and *salty* describe the subjects. Compare a linking verb with an action verb:

> The soup **tastes salty**. (*Tastes* links *soup* to the adjective *salty*.)
>
> The chef **tasted the soup**. (*Tasted* expresses an action; *soup* receives that action.)

The same verb can work differently in different sentences. Ask what the verb is doing in context.

### After the noun

Some adjectives appear after the noun, especially when they follow words such as *something, anyone,* or *nothing*, or when a longer phrase follows the noun:

> We need **something reliable**.
>
> The applicants **available for an interview** will receive an email.

This position is less common for a single everyday adjective, but it is a normal part of English sentence structure.

## 4.3 Putting adjectives in a natural order

When several adjectives describe one noun, English tends to place them in a familiar order. A useful guide is:

1. opinion or evaluation;
2. size;
3. age;
4. shape;
5. color;
6. origin or place;
7. material; and
8. purpose or type, often expressed by a noun used like an adjective.

For example:

> a **useful small old round blue Italian leather travel** bag

This example is intentionally crowded. In natural writing, most people would choose only the details the reader needs:

> She packed a **small blue travel bag**.
>
> The office bought a **large wooden conference table**.

The pattern is a tendency, not a rigid rule. Writers may change the order to create contrast or emphasis, and some adjective combinations have an established order. If several adjectives feel awkward together, do not try to force every possible detail into one noun phrase. Keep the most useful information and revise for clarity.

### Coordinate adjectives

Two adjectives are **coordinate adjectives** when they independently describe the same noun. They can often be joined by *and* or reordered naturally. A comma may separate them:

> It was a **long, difficult** meeting.
>
> It was a **difficult and long** meeting.

By contrast, adjectives that build a combined description are not usually separated by a comma:

> She works in a **small business office**.

Here, *business office* forms a combined idea, and *small* describes the office. A practical punctuation check is to see whether *and* fits between the adjectives and whether they can switch places without making the phrase sound unnatural. Chapter 14 covers commas in more detail.

## 4.4 Comparing with adjectives

Adjectives have three common **degrees of comparison**:

- **Positive:** the basic form (*clear*);
- **Comparative:** compares two people or things (*clearer*); and
- **Superlative:** identifies the highest or lowest degree in a group of three or more (*clearest*).

### The *-er/-est* pattern

Many short adjectives form the comparative with *-er* and the superlative with *-est*:

> fast → **faster** → **fastest**
>
> small → **smaller** → **smallest**

Spelling changes are common:

- For adjectives ending in silent *-e*, add *-r* or *-st*: *large, larger, largest*.
- For many consonant-vowel-consonant endings, double the final consonant: *big, bigger, biggest*; *hot, hotter, hottest*.
- For adjectives ending in consonant + *y*, change *y* to *i*: *easy, easier, easiest*.

### The *more/most* pattern

Longer adjectives usually use *more* and *most*:

> **more reliable** / **most reliable**
>
> **more important** / **most important**

Many two-syllable adjectives can use either pattern, though one form may sound more natural for a particular word or in a particular variety of English: *simpler* or *more simple*. If unsure, check a learner's dictionary and listen for the form used in reliable writing.

Do not normally use both patterns at once:

> Correct: **more useful** or **most useful**
>
> Incorrect: *more usefuler* or *most usefulest*

Also avoid adding *more* or *most* to an adjective that already has an *-er* or *-est* ending: write *faster*, not *more faster*.

### Irregular comparisons

Some common adjectives have irregular forms:

| Basic form | Comparative | Superlative |
|---|---|---|
| *good* | *better* | *best* |
| *bad* | *worse* | *worst* |
| *far* | *farther/further* | *farthest/furthest* |
| *little* (amount) | *less* | *least* |
| *many/much* | *more* | *most* |

Use *better* rather than *gooder*, and *worse* rather than *badder* in standard edited English. *Farther* often refers to physical distance; *further* often means additional or more, though the two overlap in many contexts.

### Make the comparison clear

Comparatives usually name or imply what is being compared:

> This route is **shorter than** the highway route.
>
> The second draft is **more focused**.

The second sentence leaves the comparison implied by context. Superlatives usually compare one item with a group:

> This is **the clearest example** in the chapter.

Use *than* with a comparative, not *then*:

> The new room is **larger than** the old one.

## 4.5 Participial adjectives

Some adjectives come from verb forms. These are called **participial adjectives**. Many end in *-ing* or *-ed* (or another past-participle form).

> The instructions were **confusing**.
>
> The students were **confused**.

The *-ing* form often describes the person or thing that causes a feeling or reaction. The *-ed* form often describes the person or thing experiencing it:

- a **boring** lecture (the lecture causes boredom);
- a **bored** audience (the audience feels bored);
- an **exciting** opportunity (the opportunity creates excitement);
- an **excited** applicant (the applicant feels excitement).

This is a helpful pattern, not a rule that applies to every word in the same way. Learn common pairs in context: *interesting/interested, tiring/tired, surprising/surprised,* and *satisfying/satisfied*.

### Adjective or verb form?

A form ending in *-ing* or a past participle can be an adjective or part of a verb phrase. Compare:

> The task was **confusing**. (Adjective: describes the task.)
>
> The presenter **was explaining** the task. (Verb phrase: tells what the presenter was doing.)

> The **closed** office was quiet. (Adjective: describes the office's condition.)
>
> The manager **closed** the office at six. (Verb: tells what the manager did.)

Ask whether the word describes a person or thing, or whether it helps express an action or event. In some sentences, both readings are possible; the surrounding context makes the intended meaning clear.

## 4.6 Adjectives and adverbs: *good* and *well*

An adjective describes a noun or pronoun. An adverb modifies a verb, adjective, another adverb, or a whole clause (see Chapter 1).

> That is a **good** answer. (*Good* describes the noun *answer*.)
>
> You answered **well**. (*Well* modifies the verb *answered*.)

After a linking verb, use an adjective when it describes the subject:

> The presentation **looks good**.
>
> I **feel well** today.

*Well* can be an adjective meaning “in good health.” In *I feel well*, it describes the subject *I*. Compare:

> I feel **good** about the interview. (*Good* describes my emotional state.)
>
> I feel **well** after a day of rest. (*Well* means healthy.)

Other common adjective/adverb pairs include *careful/carefully, quick/quickly,* and *clear/clearly*:

> She gave a **clear** explanation. (*Clear* describes *explanation*.)
>
> She explained the process **clearly**. (*Clearly* modifies *explained*.)

Not every adverb ends in *-ly*, and some words have the same form as an adjective and an adverb: *a fast train; the train travels fast*. Check what the word modifies.

## 4.7 Choose precise adjectives

Adjectives make writing more specific, but more adjectives do not always make a description better. Compare:

> The **very nice, really great, unbelievably wonderful** event took place in a **huge, large, big** room.
>
> The **well-organized** event took place in a **crowded** room.

The first sentence repeats similar praise and uses several intensifiers. The second gives the reader two clearer details. Choose an adjective that adds information the reader needs. Replace vague praise such as *nice* or *good* with a more specific description when possible:

- *a nice meeting* → *a focused meeting* or *a welcoming meeting*;
- *a good result* → *a measurable result* or *a reliable result*;
- *a big problem* → *a costly problem* or *a time-sensitive problem*.

Precision also depends on purpose. A lively personal reflection may need different description from a concise work update. Use only adjectives that support the point you are making.

## 4.8 Common learner challenges

### Using an adverb where an adjective is needed

After a linking verb, describe the subject with an adjective: *The plan sounds **reasonable***, not *reasonably*. Use an adverb when modifying an action: *She explained the plan **reasonably***.

### Using a double comparative or superlative

Use one comparison form: *more efficient* or *efficienter* is not a standard choice for this adjective; use *more efficient*. Do not write *most easiest*; write *easiest*.

### Comparing without a clear standard

If a comparison is important, tell readers what the adjective is compared with: *The new process is faster than the old process.*

### Confusing *-ing* and *-ed* adjectives

Check who or what causes the reaction and who experiences it: *The instructions are confusing; I am confused.*

### Adding adjectives without adding meaning

Remove repeated or vague words. One specific adjective is usually stronger than several near-synonyms.

## 4.9 Practice

### A. Add precise description

Rewrite each sentence with one or two more precise adjectives. Keep the likely meaning, but choose details that would help a reader picture or evaluate the situation. Answers will vary.

1. We held a **good** meeting in a **nice** room.
2. The team created a **big** change in the process.
3. I read an **interesting** report about a **bad** problem.
4. The company offers a **good** training program for new employees.

### B. Choose or form the correct comparison

1. This route is (**shorter / more short**) than the one we took yesterday.
2. Of the three proposals, this one is (**the most practical / the practicalest**).
3. The second explanation was (**clearer / more clearer**) than the first.
4. The new software is (**more reliable / reliabler**) than the old version.
5. This is the (**better / best**) result we have achieved so far.
6. The library is (**farther / more far**) from the station than the café.
7. This week has been (**busier / more busy**) than last week.
8. The assignment was (**less difficult / least difficult**) than I expected. (Compare the assignment with your expectation, not with every assignment.)

### C. Adjective or adverb?

Choose the correct form.

1. The instructions were (**clear / clearly**).
2. The technician explained the instructions (**clear / clearly**).
3. We had a (**good / well**) discussion.
4. The team worked (**good / well**) under pressure.
5. The soup smells (**delicious / deliciously**).
6. The chef smelled the soup (**careful / carefully**).
7. The participants felt (**excited / excitedly**) about the workshop.
8. They responded (**polite / politely**) to the feedback.

### D. Edit inflated or unclear descriptions

Revise the paragraph by removing repetition, replacing vague adjectives with more useful details, correcting any adjective or adverb error, and improving the comparison. There are at least five improvements.

> The new online portal is a really great and very useful system. It is more easier to use than the old one, and it loads very quickly and fast. The homepage has a big, large, huge menu with a nice, good design. Students can find the important forms more easy than before.

### E. Apply it

Describe a study space, service, process, or recent experience in four or five sentences. Use:

- at least two adjectives before nouns;
- at least one adjective after a linking verb;
- one comparative or superlative; and
- one adjective/adverb pair used correctly.

Underline the adjectives and circle the nouns or pronouns they describe. Then remove any adjective that does not add useful information.

## 4.10 Answer key

### A. Add precise description

Answers will vary. Sample revisions:

1. We held a **focused** meeting in a **quiet** room.
2. The team created a **substantial** change in the process.
3. I read a **detailed** report about a **costly** problem.
4. The company offers a **hands-on** training program for new employees.

The best adjective depends on what the writer means. Avoid replacing a vague word with another vague word; choose a detail that is accurate and relevant.

### B. Choose or form the correct comparison

1. **shorter** — A common short adjective takes *-er*.
2. **the most practical** — This longer adjective usually takes *most*.
3. **clearer** — Do not use both *more* and *-er*.
4. **more reliable** — This adjective usually takes *more*.
5. **best** — *Good* has the irregular superlative *best*.
6. **farther** — It refers to physical distance here. *Further* is also used for distance by many speakers, but *more far* is not the usual form.
7. **busier** — Change consonant + *y* to *-ier*.
8. **less difficult** — The sentence compares the assignment with an expectation; it does not say it is the least difficult in a group. *Less* gives the intended comparison.

### C. Adjective or adverb?

1. **clear** — It describes the instructions after the linking verb *were*.
2. **clearly** — It modifies the action *explained*.
3. **good** — It describes the noun *discussion*.
4. **well** — It modifies the action *worked*.
5. **delicious** — It describes the soup after the linking verb *smells*.
6. **carefully** — It modifies the action *smelled*.
7. **excited** — It describes how the participants felt.
8. **politely** — It modifies the action *responded*.

### D. Edit inflated or unclear descriptions

Answers will vary. One possible revision is:

> The new online portal is a **useful** system. It is **easier** to use than the old one and loads **quickly**. Its homepage has a **clear menu** and an **uncluttered design**. Students can find the required forms **more easily** than before.

This revision removes repeated praise (*really great and very useful; big, large, huge; nice, good*), removes the double comparative *more easier*, replaces *quickly and fast* with one adverb, and changes *more easy* to *more easily* because the word modifies *find*. *Required* and *uncluttered* give more specific information than *important* and *nice* in this context.

### E. Apply it

Answers will vary. Check that the adjectives describe nouns or pronouns, that the comparison is formed correctly, and that adverbs modify actions or other modifiers. Remove descriptions that repeat information or do not support the purpose.

## Chapter summary

Adjectives describe or limit nouns and pronouns. They commonly appear before nouns or after linking verbs, and sometimes after the noun they describe. When several adjectives appear together, English tends toward a natural order, but the pattern is flexible; clear writing usually uses only the details the reader needs. Comparatives compare two things, and superlatives identify the highest or lowest degree in a group. Short adjectives often use *-er/-est*, longer adjectives often use *more/most*, and some forms are irregular. Participial adjectives such as *confusing* and *confused* describe qualities or reactions, while similar-looking verb forms can express actions. Finally, choose adjectives and adverbs according to what they modify: a *good* answer, an answer given *well*.
