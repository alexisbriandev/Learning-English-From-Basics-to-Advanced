# Chapter 8. Conjunctions

## What you will learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- explain how conjunctions connect words, phrases, and clauses;
- choose a coordinating conjunction to show addition, contrast, choice, result, or another relationship;
- use correlative pairs and keep the connected elements parallel;
- recognize subordinating conjunctions and the dependent clauses they introduce;
- punctuate common conjunction patterns; and
- repair comma splices, fragments, mismatched pairs, and faulty parallel structure.

## 8.1 What is a conjunction?

A **conjunction** connects words, phrases, or clauses. A conjunction helps readers understand how two ideas relate: Are they added, contrasted, offered as choices, or linked by time, cause, or condition?

> We ordered tea **and** coffee. (Connects words.)
>
> She works **in the morning and after lunch**. (Connects phrases.)
>
> The meeting ended, **but** the team stayed to talk. (Connects clauses.)

A **clause** is a group of words organized around a subject and a verb. An **independent clause** can stand as a complete sentence; a **dependent clause** cannot stand alone in the same way. Chapter 12 explains phrases and clauses in more detail, and Chapter 13 focuses on sentence structure.

## 8.2 Coordinating conjunctions

**Coordinating conjunctions** connect elements of equal grammatical importance: word to word, phrase to phrase, or independent clause to independent clause. The seven common coordinators are often remembered with the mnemonic **FANBOYS**:

| Conjunction | Common relationship | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **for** | reason or explanation; often formal or literary | We left early, **for** the roads were icy. |
| **and** | addition or sequence | She reviewed the budget **and** updated the schedule. |
| **nor** | a second negative idea | He did not call, **nor** did he send an email. |
| **but** | contrast | The room was small, **but** it was quiet. |
| **or** | choice or alternative | You can meet online **or** in person. |
| **yet** | contrast or unexpected result | The task was difficult, **yet** the team finished on time. |
| **so** | result or consequence | The bus was delayed, **so** we took a taxi. |

The relationship between ideas should guide your choice. *And* adds information; *but* signals contrast; *so* introduces a result. Do not choose a conjunction simply because it sounds familiar if it creates the wrong relationship.

### Punctuation with coordinating conjunctions

When a coordinator joins two **independent clauses**, place a comma before the conjunction:

> The library closes at six, **so** we should leave soon.

Both parts can stand alone: *The library closes at six.* / *We should leave soon.*

Do not normally put a comma before a conjunction that joins only words or phrases:

> Bring a notebook **and** a pen.
>
> We can meet **in the office or online**.

Short clauses joined by *and* or *but* may not need a comma when the connection is simple, though a comma can help if it improves clarity. Chapter 14 covers comma choices in more detail.

### *For* and *so*: meaning and style

As conjunctions, *for* can introduce a reason and *so* can introduce a result:

> We postponed the event, **for** the speaker was ill. (The speaker's illness is the reason.)
>
> The speaker was ill, **so** we postponed the event. (Postponing is the result.)

In everyday writing, *because* is usually more natural than conjunction *for* when giving a reason. *For* also has other uses as a preposition, as in *a note for you*.

### Avoid a comma splice

A **comma splice** occurs when a comma alone joins two independent clauses. A coordinating conjunction can repair it:

> Comma splice: The room was unavailable, we met online.
>
> Correct: The room was unavailable, **so** we met online.

Other repairs include using a period or a semicolon. See Chapter 13 for sentence-boundary patterns and Chapter 14 for punctuation.

## 8.3 Correlative conjunctions

**Correlative conjunctions** work in pairs. They connect equal or parallel elements in a sentence.

| Pair | Example |
|---|---|
| **either … or** | You can **either** call **or** send a message. |
| **neither … nor** | The report is **neither** complete **nor** accurate. |
| **both … and** | The role requires **both** patience **and** attention to detail. |
| **not only … but also** | The workshop was **not only** practical **but also** engaging. |

Keep the two parts of the pair together and make sure each pair is complete:

> Correct: **Either** we revise the draft **or** we submit it as it is.
>
> Incorrect: *Either* we revise the draft *and* submit it as it is.

### Keep parallel structure

The elements joined by a correlative pair should have the same grammatical form. This matching is called **parallel structure**.

> Parallel: The course teaches students **to research, to write, and to present**.
>
> Not parallel: The course teaches students **to research, writing, and how to present**.

> Parallel: She is **both organized and patient**.
>
> Not parallel: She is **both organized and has patience**.

In the first pair, all three activities use infinitive phrases. In the second, both connected qualities are adjectives. Parallel forms make a sentence easier to read and compare.

With *not only … but also*, place each part of the pair immediately before the parallel element it connects:

> The new policy **not only saves time but also reduces errors**.

The sentence connects two verb phrases: *saves time* and *reduces errors*.

### Correlative pairs and agreement

When *either/or* or *neither/nor* connects two subjects, verb agreement can depend on the nearer subject in standard edited usage. A sentence may sound awkward if the subjects differ in number, so consider rewriting it:

> Awkward: Either the managers or the assistant **is** presenting.
>
> Clearer: Either the assistant is presenting, or the managers are presenting.

Chapter 11 discusses agreement in more detail.

## 8.4 Subordinating conjunctions

A **subordinating conjunction** introduces a dependent clause and shows how that clause relates to an independent clause. The relationship may involve time, cause, condition, contrast, or purpose.

| Relationship | Common subordinating conjunctions | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Time | *after, before, when, while, once, until, since* | **After the seminar ended**, we went for lunch. |
| Cause or reason | *because, since, as* | We used the side entrance **because the main door was locked**. |
| Condition | *if, unless, provided that* | **If the room is available**, we will meet there. |
| Contrast or concession | *although, though, even though, whereas, while* | **Although the process takes longer**, it reduces errors. |
| Purpose | *so that* | She wrote the steps down **so that everyone could follow them**. |

The bolded dependent clauses cannot normally stand alone as complete sentences in this use. They need an independent clause to complete the thought:

> **Because the main door was locked** … (The reader expects more.)
>
> We used the side entrance **because the main door was locked**. (Complete sentence.)

Some words have more than one grammatical use. *Since* can introduce time (*since the term began*) or a reason (*since the room is full*). Choose a sentence structure that makes your meaning clear.

## 8.5 Punctuation with dependent clauses

Punctuation depends on where the dependent clause appears and how it relates to the independent clause.

### Dependent clause first: usually use a comma

When a sentence begins with a dependent clause, place a comma after it:

> **When the meeting ends**, please turn off the projector.
>
> **Although the instructions were brief**, the team understood the process.

### Independent clause first: usually no comma

When the independent clause comes first and the dependent clause follows, a comma is usually unnecessary:

> Please turn off the projector **when the meeting ends**.
>
> The team understood the process **although the instructions were brief**.

The comma pattern may change if the dependent clause is nonessential or if another punctuation rule applies. Chapter 14 covers commas; Chapter 13 explains how clauses combine into sentences.

### Do not confuse dependent and independent clauses

> **Because the room was booked**, we met online. (Dependent clause + comma + independent clause.)
>
> The room was booked, **so we met online**. (Independent clause + comma + coordinator + independent clause.)

The first uses a subordinating conjunction, *because*. The second uses a coordinating conjunction, *so*. A comma alone cannot correctly join the two independent clauses.

## 8.6 Common problems

### Comma splices

Do not join two independent clauses with only a comma:

> Incorrect: The deadline changed, we updated the schedule.
>
> Correct: The deadline changed, **so** we updated the schedule.

### Sentence fragments

A dependent clause by itself is usually a fragment in standard edited prose:

> Fragment: **Although the train was delayed.**
>
> Complete: **Although the train was delayed, we arrived before the meeting.**

Fragments can be used deliberately in some informal or creative writing, but academic and professional sentences generally need a complete independent clause. See Chapter 13 for more on fragments.

### Mismatched correlative pairs

Use the correct partners: *either/or, neither/nor, both/and,* and *not only/but also*. Do not mix pair members.

### Faulty parallelism

Keep the connected elements in the same grammatical form:

> Not parallel: The job involves **planning events, to answer emails, and meetings with clients**.
>
> Parallel: The job involves **planning events, answering emails, and meeting with clients**.

### Choosing a connector that misstates the relationship

If one event is a result of another, use a result connector such as *so*. If the second idea contrasts with the first, use *but* or *although*. If it explains a reason, use *because*. A grammatically correct conjunction can still create a logical error if it signals the wrong relationship.

### Overloading a sentence with connectors

Several connectors in one sentence can make the logic difficult to follow. Use only the connections the reader needs, and divide a long sentence when it contains several separate points.

## 8.7 Practice

### A. Combine short ideas

Combine each pair into one sentence using the conjunction in parentheses. Add punctuation where needed. More than one word order may work.

1. The lab was closed. We worked in the library. (*so*)
2. The speaker was nervous. She answered every question. (*but*)
3. You can submit the form online. You can bring it to the office. (*either … or*)
4. The course is practical. The course is challenging. (*both … and*)
5. We will begin the interview. The applicant arrives. (*when*)
6. The team saved a backup. The system might fail. (*because*)
7. The project takes longer. The new method is more accurate. (*although*)
8. The assistant checked the dates. The assistant confirmed the room. (*and*)

### B. Select a logical connector

Choose the best connector from the choices. Explain the relationship it signals.

1. The bus was delayed, (**so / although**) I joined the meeting online.
2. (**Because / But**) the room was unavailable, we moved the workshop.
3. (**Although / So**) the report was short, it included all the key results.
4. You can (**either / both**) email the receipt **or / and** upload it to the portal.
5. The instructions were clear, (**yet / for**) several people asked for help.
6. We can finish today (**if / because**) everyone sends their section by noon.

### C. Repair the sentences

Correct each sentence. Some have more than one problem.

1. The printer stopped working, we called technical support.
2. Because the room was too small.
3. She is responsible not only for the budget but also manages the schedule.
4. The program teaches students to plan, writing, and how to present.
5. Neither the coordinator or the instructor has the updated list.
6. I checked the figures and, I sent the report.

### D. Revise the paragraph

Revise the paragraph to reduce choppiness, repair run-ons or fragments, correct conjunction use, and improve parallel structure. Aim for a clear, practical account of the event.

> The orientation began late. The room was unavailable. We waited in the lobby, the coordinator checked another room. Because the projector was not working. The team moved the presentation online but the participants could not see the slides. We not only emailed a link but also sending a copy of the slides. The session ran short, we answered the remaining questions by email.

### E. Apply it

Write a short paragraph about a study or work task. Use:

- one coordinating conjunction joining independent clauses;
- one correlative pair with parallel elements; and
- one subordinating conjunction.

Underline each conjunction and bracket the clauses it connects. Check that the punctuation reflects whether each clause is independent or dependent.

## 8.8 Answer key

### A. Combine short ideas

Answers may vary. Sample answers:

1. **The lab was closed, so we worked in the library.** *So* introduces the result.
2. **The speaker was nervous, but she answered every question.** *But* signals contrast.
3. **You can either submit the form online or bring it to the office.** *Either/or* connects two verb phrases.
4. **The course is both practical and challenging.** *Both/and* connects two adjectives.
5. **We will begin the interview when the applicant arrives.** The dependent time clause follows the independent clause, so no comma is usually needed.
6. **We saved a backup because the system might fail.** *Because* gives the reason.
7. **Although the project takes longer, the new method is more accurate.** The dependent clause comes first, so use a comma after it.
8. **The assistant checked the dates and confirmed the room.** The shared subject allows a concise compound predicate; no comma is needed.

### B. Select a logical connector

1. **so** — The delay caused the speaker to join online.
2. **Because** — The unavailable room is the reason for moving the workshop.
3. **Although** — The short length contrasts with the report's completeness.
4. **either … or** — These words present two alternatives.
5. **yet** — The requests for help contrast with the clear instructions. *For* would give a reason and change the meaning.
6. **if** — Sending each section by noon is a condition for finishing today.

### C. Repair the sentences

1. **The printer stopped working, so we called technical support.** The conjunction connects two independent clauses and signals a result.
2. **Because the room was too small, we moved the meeting online.** Add an independent clause to complete the dependent clause.
3. **She is responsible not only for the budget but also for managing the schedule.** The pair connects two prepositional phrases: *for the budget* and *for managing the schedule*. Another revision is **She is responsible not only for the budget but also for the schedule.**
4. **The program teaches students to plan, to write, and to present.** The three connected elements use parallel infinitive phrases.
5. **Neither the coordinator nor the instructor has the updated list.** The correct pair is *neither/nor*. (The subjects are both singular, so *has* is appropriate.)
6. **I checked the figures, and I sent the report.** The comma belongs before *and* because it joins two independent clauses. A concise alternative is **I checked the figures and sent the report**, with one shared subject.

### D. Revise the paragraph

Answers will vary. One possible revision is:

> The orientation began late because the room was unavailable. While the coordinator looked for another room, we waited in the lobby. The projector was not working, so the team moved the presentation online; however, participants could not see the slides. We not only emailed a link but also sent a copy of the slides. Because the session ran short, we answered the remaining questions by email.

This version attaches the reason about the room to a complete sentence, repairs the comma splice, connects the projector problem to the response, keeps the *not only … but also* elements parallel (*emailed* and *sent*), and completes the final reason clause. The semicolon before *however* joins two independent clauses; a period would also work.

### E. Apply it

Answers will vary. Check that the coordinating conjunction joins equivalent elements, the correlative pair is complete and parallel, the subordinating conjunction introduces a dependent clause, and commas appear where needed.

## Chapter summary

Conjunctions connect words, phrases, and clauses. Coordinating conjunctions (*for, and, nor, but, or, yet,* and *so*) join elements of equal grammatical importance and signal relationships such as addition, contrast, choice, reason, or result. Correlative pairs connect matching elements and work best with parallel structure. Subordinating conjunctions introduce dependent clauses that express time, cause, condition, contrast, or purpose. Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction when it joins two independent clauses, and usually use a comma after a dependent clause that begins a sentence. Avoid comma splices, sentence fragments, mismatched pairs, faulty parallelism, and connectors that misrepresent the logic.
