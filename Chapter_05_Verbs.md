# Chapter 5. Verbs

## What you will learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- identify verbs as words that express actions, events, states, or relationships;
- distinguish main verbs from helping verbs and recognize verb phrases;
- form common regular and irregular verb forms;
- choose verb forms to show time and aspect;
- use modal verbs for ability, possibility, obligation, advice, and polite requests;
- recognize and form active and passive voice;
- tell whether a verb takes an object; and
- correct common verb problems, including tense shifts and missing helping verbs.

## 5.1 What is a verb?

A **verb** expresses an action, event, state, or relationship. It is the central part of a sentence's **predicate**—the part that says something about the subject.

- Action: The analyst **checked** the figures.
- Event: The workshop **began** at nine.
- State: I **understand** the instructions.
- Relationship: The results **are** accurate.

The verb tells what the subject does, what happens, or what condition or relationship applies. In a complete statement, a verb or verb phrase helps anchor the sentence:

> The intern **organized** the files.
>
> The intern **has been organizing** the files.

The second example has three verb words working together as one verb phrase. The verb forms help communicate not only what happened but also how the action relates to time.

## 5.2 Main verbs and helping verbs

A **main verb** carries the central meaning. A **helping verb**, also called an **auxiliary verb**, works with a main verb to form questions, negatives, time and aspect, emphasis, or voice.

> The team **is preparing** a budget.

*Preparing* is the main verb; *is* is a helping verb. Together they make the verb phrase *is preparing*.

The primary helping verbs are forms of **be**, **have**, and **do**:

- Forms of *be*: *am, is, are, was, were, be, being, been*.
- Forms of *have*: *have, has, had*.
- Forms of *do*: *do, does, did*.

Helping verbs have several common jobs:

- **Progressive aspect:** *She **is reviewing** the application.*
- **Perfect aspect:** *She **has reviewed** the application.*
- **Passive voice:** *The application **was reviewed** yesterday.*
- **Questions:** ***Did** she review the application?*
- **Negatives:** *She **does not** review applications.*

### Questions and negatives with *do*

In the simple present and past, English often uses *do, does,* or *did* to form questions and negatives when there is no other helping verb:

> **Do** you need a copy? / I **do not** need a copy.
>
> **Does** the office open at eight? / It **does not** open until nine.
>
> **Did** they call yesterday? / They **did not** call.

After *do, does,* or *did*, use the base form of the main verb: *Does she **work** here?* Not *Does she works here?*

If a helping verb is already present, it usually forms the question or negative without *do*:

> **Are** you waiting? / You **are not** waiting.
>
> **Has** the meeting started? / The meeting **has not** started.

## 5.3 Common verb forms

English verbs have several forms. A verb may change spelling to show person or time, or it may use a helping verb.

| Form | Meaning or use | Example with *review* |
|---|---|---|
| Base form | Basic form; used after *to*, modals, and some helping verbs | *review* |
| Third-person singular present | Present form with *he, she, it,* or a singular noun | *reviews* |
| Past | Usually describes a completed past event | *reviewed* |
| Past participle | Used with *have* for perfect forms and with *be* in passive forms | *reviewed* |
| *-ing* form | Used with forms of *be* in progressive phrases; also appears in other structures | *reviewing* |

### Regular verbs

Most verbs form the past and past participle by adding *-ed* or *-d*. These are **regular verbs**:

> work → worked → worked
>
> arrive → arrived → arrived

Common spelling patterns include:

- Add *-d* to verbs ending in silent *-e*: *live/lived*.
- For many verbs ending in consonant + *y*, change *y* to *i* and add *-ed*: *study/studied*.
- For some short verbs ending in consonant-vowel-consonant, double the last consonant: *plan/planned*. The pattern depends partly on stress and spelling conventions; check an unfamiliar form.

The third-person singular present usually adds *-s* or *-es*: *work/works, watch/watches*. A verb ending in consonant + *y* changes to *-ies*: *study/studies*. These endings matter in sentences such as *She works nearby* and *He studies at night*.

### Irregular verbs

**Irregular verbs** do not form their past or past participle with the usual *-ed* pattern. Some change both forms; others keep the same form:

| Base | Past | Past participle |
|---|---|---|
| *be* | *was/were* | *been* |
| *begin* | *began* | *begun* |
| *bring* | *brought* | *brought* |
| *buy* | *bought* | *bought* |
| *choose* | *chose* | *chosen* |
| *do* | *did* | *done* |
| *eat* | *ate* | *eaten* |
| *go* | *went* | *gone* |
| *make* | *made* | *made* |
| *see* | *saw* | *seen* |
| *take* | *took* | *taken* |
| *write* | *wrote* | *written* |

Learn irregular forms as you meet them. A learner's dictionary usually lists the past and past participle forms.

### Do not confuse the past form and past participle

For regular verbs, the past and past participle look the same (*finished/finished*). For many irregular verbs, they differ:

> We **went** to the orientation yesterday. (*Went* is the past form.)
>
> We have **gone** to the orientation. (*Gone* is the past participle after *have*.)

> She **wrote** the report. (*Wrote* is the past form.)
>
> She has **written** the report. (*Written* is the past participle after *has*.)

## 5.4 Time and aspect

Verb forms help readers understand **time**: when an event happens in relation to the moment of speaking or another time. They also show **aspect**: how the speaker views an event—as a habit, a completed whole, an ongoing action, or an action connected to another time.

The examples below use the same situation to show common choices. Actual meaning depends on context, and English often uses more than one form to refer to future time.

### Present-time forms

| Form | Common use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple present | Habits, routines, facts, and states | *I **check** email each morning.* / *Water **boils** at 100°C at sea level.* |
| Present progressive (*be + -ing*) | An action happening now or a temporary situation | *I **am checking** the figures now.* / *She **is working** from home this week.* |
| Present perfect (*have/has + past participle*) | A past action connected to now, or experience with no stated finished time | *We **have completed** the first section.* / *He **has visited** the campus before.* |
| Present perfect progressive (*have/has been + -ing*) | An activity that began earlier and continues now, or whose duration/recent activity matters | *They **have been preparing** the report for two days.* |

Do not normally use the present perfect with a specific, finished past-time expression such as *yesterday* or *in 2023*:

> I **submitted** the form yesterday.
>
> I **have submitted** the form. (The exact time is not stated; the result matters now.)

### Past-time forms

| Form | Common use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple past | A completed event at a past time | *We **met** the client last week.* |
| Past progressive (*was/were + -ing*) | An action in progress at a past moment; often provides background | *We **were meeting** the client when the system went offline.* |
| Past perfect (*had + past participle*) | An event completed before another past event or time | *We **had sent** the draft before the client called.* |
| Past perfect progressive (*had been + -ing*) | Duration or ongoing activity up to a past point | *We **had been waiting** for an hour when the office opened.* |

Use the past perfect when the order of past events needs to be clear. If the sequence is already clear or events are told in order, the simple past may be enough.

### Future meaning

English commonly talks about future events with *will*, *be going to*, and the present progressive. These forms have different typical uses:

- **Will + base form:** predictions, offers, promises, and decisions made as you speak: *I **will send** the file after this call.*
- **Be going to + base form:** plans or intentions already formed, or predictions based on present evidence: *We **are going to revise** the schedule.* / *That box **is going to fall**.*
- **Present progressive:** an arranged future event: *I **am meeting** the adviser at two.*

Other forms can refer to the future too. The simple present often describes fixed schedules: *The train **leaves** at 6:15.* Choose a form that fits the context rather than treating one pattern as the only future form.

### Progressive and perfect forms with future meaning

Future expressions can combine with progressive or perfect aspect:

> At this time tomorrow, we **will be presenting** the proposal. (The presentation will be in progress.)
>
> By Friday, we **will have finished** the draft. (The draft will be complete before a future point.)

These forms are useful when the timeline matters. In everyday messages, a simpler future form is often enough.

### Keep a timeline in view

Before choosing a verb form, ask:

1. When does the event happen: now, before now, or later?
2. Is it a habit, a completed event, or an action in progress?
3. Does it connect to another time, and if so, which event happened first?
4. What does the reader need to know: the action, its duration, or its result?

## 5.5 Modal verbs

**Modal verbs** are helping verbs that express ideas such as ability, possibility, permission, obligation, advice, prediction, or willingness. The modals covered here are *can, could, may, might, must, should, will,* and *would*.

| Modal | Common meanings | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *can* | Ability; informal permission; possibility | *I **can** attend the meeting.* |
| *could* | Past ability; possibility; polite request | ***Could** you send the link?* |
| *may* | Permission; possibility | ***May** I leave early?* / *The delay **may** affect delivery.* |
| *might* | Possibility, often less certain | *We **might** need another day.* |
| *must* | Strong obligation or logical conclusion | *Visitors **must** sign in.* / *The lights are on; someone **must** be inside.* |
| *should* | Advice, expectation, or a recommended action | *You **should** save a backup.* |
| *will* | Future meaning, willingness, or a promise | *I **will** update the team.* |
| *would* | Polite requests, imagined situations, or repeated past actions | ***Would** you review this draft?* |

Use a modal before the base form of the main verb. Do not add *-s* to the main verb after a modal:

> She **can join** the call. (Not *can joins*.)
>
> He **should review** the figures. (Not *should reviews*.)

Modals do not take *to* before the main verb: *might leave*, not *might to leave*. One exception in form is *ought to*: *You ought to ask first.*

### Ability, possibility, and permission

> I **can** use the design software. (Ability.)
>
> The delivery **could/might** arrive today. (Possibility.)
>
> **May** I borrow the projector? (Polite request for permission.)

### Obligation and advice

> Staff **must** wear identification badges. (Strong requirement.)
>
> You **should** confirm the room before sending invitations. (Advice.)

The negative forms can have different meanings. *Must not* usually means something is prohibited; *do not have to* means it is not required:

> You **must not** share the access code. (Do not share it.)
>
> You **do not have to** print the form. (Printing is optional.)

### Polite requests

Modal choice affects tone. *Can you…?* is common and direct. *Could you…?* and *Would you…?* often sound more tentative or polite:

> **Could you send** the updated file when you have a moment?
>
> **Would you mind reviewing** the first page? (*Would you mind* is followed by an *-ing* form.)

## 5.6 Active and passive voice

**Voice** shows how a sentence presents the relationship between the subject and the action.

In **active voice**, the subject performs the action:

> The facilities team **repaired** the elevator.

In **passive voice**, the subject receives the action. The usual form is a form of *be* + the past participle:

> The elevator **was repaired** by the facilities team.

To form the passive, use the appropriate form of *be* and the past participle of the main verb. The tense is carried by *be*:

- Present: *The forms **are reviewed** each morning.*
- Past: *The forms **were reviewed** yesterday.*
- Present perfect: *The forms **have been reviewed**.*
- Future: *The forms **will be reviewed** tomorrow.*

The person or thing performing the action can be added in a **by-phrase**, but it can be omitted when it is unknown, obvious, or unimportant:

> The laptop **was found** in the conference room. (The finder is not important or known.)
>
> The laptop **was found by a visitor**. (The finder matters.)

### When to use each voice

Active voice is usually direct and easy to follow when you know who did the action:

> The research team analyzed the survey results.

Passive voice is useful when the receiver or result is the focus, or when the agent is unknown or not relevant:

> The survey results were analyzed before the presentation.

Passive voice is common in process descriptions and formal reports. It can also hide responsibility when the reader needs to know who acted. Prefer active voice when it makes the sentence clearer, and use passive voice when its focus serves the reader.

## 5.7 Transitive and intransitive verbs

A **transitive verb** has an object: a noun or pronoun that receives the action or completes the verb's meaning.

> She **updated the calendar**.

*Updated* is transitive here. *The calendar* is its object.

An **intransitive verb** does not take a direct object:

> The meeting **ended** at noon.

*Ended* has no direct object. The phrase *at noon* tells when; it is not an object.

Some verbs can be transitive or intransitive in different sentences:

> The assistant **opened the window**. (Transitive; *the window* is the object.)
>
> The window **opened** slowly. (Intransitive; no direct object.)

Some verbs need an object to complete their meaning in a particular use: *She placed the folder…* leaves the reader asking where, while *She placed the folder on the desk* is complete. Other verbs can be used with or without an object depending on context: *We ate lunch* / *We ate.*

Some transitive verbs can take two objects:

> The manager **sent the staff an update**.

*The staff* receives the update; *an update* is the thing sent. A later chapter will explore object roles in more detail.

## 5.8 Common learner challenges

### Shifting tense without a reason

Keep the time frame consistent unless the meaning changes:

> In the morning, I **checked** the room and **tested** the projector. (Past events.)
>
> In the morning, I **checked** the room and **test** the projector. (Unexplained shift.)

### Leaving out a helping verb

Progressive, perfect, and passive forms need their helping verbs:

> The team **is preparing** the slides. (Not *The team preparing the slides* as a complete statement.)
>
> The form **was submitted** yesterday. (Not *The form submitted yesterday* if the intended meaning is passive.)

### Using the wrong irregular form

Check whether a sentence needs the past form or the past participle:

> We **went** to the session. / We have **gone** to the session.
>
> She **wrote** the summary. / She has **written** the summary.

### Adding an ending after a modal or *does/did*

Use the base form after these helping verbs:

> He **does work** remotely. / **Does** he **work** remotely?
>
> She **can finish** today. (Not *can finishes*.)

### Overusing the passive voice

If the reader needs to know who acted, name that person or team: *The analyst corrected the error* is clearer than *The error was corrected* when responsibility matters.

## 5.10 Answer key

### A. Choose verb forms for a timeline

1. **reviews** — Simple present for a recurring routine; singular *team* takes *-s* here.
2. **am uploading** — Present progressive for an action happening now.
3. **approved** — Simple past with the finished time expression *yesterday afternoon*.
4. **have finished** — Present perfect because the completed action has a result now.
5. **was meeting** — Past progressive for the ongoing activity interrupted by the alarm.
6. **had sent** — Past perfect for the proposal sent before the client called.
7. **have been working** — Present perfect progressive for an activity that began earlier and continues now.
8. **leaves** — Simple present for a fixed schedule.
9. **will save** — *Will* expresses a prediction.
10. **will be presenting** — Future progressive for an action in progress at a particular future time.

### B. Revise the process description

> First, the coordinator **checks** the request for missing details. If information is incomplete, the coordinator **contacts** the applicant. After the request is complete, the staff members **review** it and **enter** the details into the tracking system. The system **sends** a confirmation email, and the coordinator **files** the request in the shared folder.

The routine uses simple present throughout. The six changes are *checked → checks, contacted → contacts, reviews → review, entered → enter, send → sends,* and *is filing → files*. *Staff members* is plural, so it takes *review*.

### C. Choose the right modal

1. **Could** — A polite request. *Would you forward…?* is also possible.
2. **must not** — This prohibits sharing. *Do not have to* would mean sharing is optional, not forbidden.
3. **might** — Attendance is uncertain, so the need is possible rather than certain.
4. **should** — This gives a recommendation.
5. **May** — This politely asks for permission. *Could I use…?* is also common.

### D. Active or passive?

1. **The printer was repaired by the technician this morning.**
2. **The finance team prepares the weekly report.**
3. **The meeting has been moved to Room 204.** (The agent can be added: *Someone has moved…* is the active form.)
4. **The committee will review the applications.**

### E. Write a polite request and a status update

Answers will vary. Check that the request uses a polite modal and a base-form verb (for example, *Could you review…?*). The status update should distinguish completed work from work in progress or a future step and give a clear time reference.

## Chapter summary

Verbs express actions, events, states, or relationships and form the center of a sentence's predicate. Main verbs carry the central meaning; helping verbs form questions, negatives, voice, and time/aspect combinations. Common verb forms include the base, third-person singular present, past, past participle, and *-ing* form. Regular verbs usually add *-ed* for the past and past participle; irregular verbs must be learned. Simple, progressive, and perfect forms show habits or whole events, ongoing actions, and connections between times. Modals express meanings such as possibility, obligation, advice, and polite requests. Active voice foregrounds the doer; passive voice foregrounds the receiver or result. Transitive verbs take objects, while intransitive verbs do not. Clear writing keeps verb forms consistent, includes required helping verbs, and chooses active or passive voice to suit the reader's needs.
