# Chapter 13. Sentences

## What you will learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- recognize a complete sentence and identify its subject and predicate;
- explain the roles of objects and complements;
- distinguish sentence purposes and sentence structures;
- use coordination and subordination to show relationships between ideas;
- diagnose and repair fragments, run-ons, and comma splices; and
- revise sentences for variety, emphasis, and concision.

## 13.1 What makes a complete sentence?

In standard writing, a complete sentence begins with a capital letter and ends with appropriate punctuation. Its core is at least one **independent clause**: a group of words with a subject and a verb that expresses a complete thought.

> **The workshop begins at nine.**

The subject is *the workshop*. The verb is *begins*. Together they form an independent clause that can stand as a sentence.

A group of words can have a subject and verb but still not be a complete sentence if it is dependent on another clause:

> **Because the workshop begins at nine.** (Not complete in this use; the word *because* makes the clause dependent.)
>
> **Because the workshop begins at nine, we should arrive early.** (Complete sentence: dependent clause + independent clause.)

Punctuation marks the sentence's boundaries, but a capital letter and period cannot make an incomplete thought complete. The sentence also needs a sound grammatical structure and a complete main idea.

## 13.2 Subject, predicate, object, and complement

### Subject and predicate

The **subject** tells who or what the sentence is about. The **predicate** says something about the subject and includes the verb and the words connected to it.

> **The two new interns** **organized the shared files before lunch**.

The subject is *the two new interns*. The predicate is *organized the shared files before lunch*. The subject's main noun is *interns*; *two* and *new* give more information about them.

### Objects

An **object** is a noun or pronoun that receives an action or completes the meaning of a verb.

> The interns organized **the shared files**.
>
> The manager sent **them** **a reminder**.

In the first sentence, *the shared files* is the direct object of *organized*. In the second, *them* is the indirect object (the recipients) and *a reminder* is the direct object (what was sent).

### Complements

A **complement** completes the meaning of a subject or object. A subject complement often follows a linking verb and identifies or describes the subject:

> The room is **available**. (Adjective describing the subject.)
>
> Mina became **the team lead**. (Noun phrase identifying the subject.)

An object complement describes or renames an object:

> The committee named Mina **team lead**.

Here *team lead* identifies the object *Mina*. You will see objects and complements used in different sentence structures throughout this chapter.

## 13.3 Four sentence purposes

Sentences can be grouped by what the writer or speaker is doing. A sentence's **purpose** is different from its structure. The same structure can serve different purposes.

### Declarative: making a statement

A **declarative sentence** states information and usually ends with a period:

> The registration deadline is Friday.

### Interrogative: asking a question

An **interrogative sentence** asks a direct question and ends with a question mark:

> Is the registration deadline Friday?
>
> When is the registration deadline?

An indirect question is usually part of a declarative sentence and does not use a question mark by itself:

> I wonder **when the registration deadline is**.

### Imperative: giving a direction or request

An **imperative sentence** gives an instruction, direction, invitation, or request. Its subject is usually the understood pronoun *you*:

> Please submit the form by Friday. (You submit it.)
>
> Take a seat near the window. (You take a seat.)

Imperatives usually end with a period. A question mark or exclamation point may be appropriate depending on the intended tone:

> Would you please submit the form by Friday? (Polite request phrased as a question.)
>
> Watch out! (Urgent warning.)

### Exclamatory: expressing strong feeling

An **exclamatory sentence** expresses strong feeling or emphasis and often ends with an exclamation point:

> What a useful guide this is!
>
> We finished ahead of schedule!

Use exclamation points with purpose, especially in professional or academic writing. A sentence can express excitement without an exclamation point: *We finished ahead of schedule. The results look excellent.*

## 13.4 Four sentence structures

Sentence structure depends on the number and kind of clauses—not on how long the sentence is or how many commas it contains. The four common patterns are simple, compound, complex, and compound-complex.

### Simple sentence: one independent clause

A **simple sentence** has one independent clause. It may have a compound subject, a compound verb, or many phrases and still be simple.

> **The new schedule begins Monday.**
>
> **The manager and assistant reviewed the draft and approved the changes.**

The second sentence has two subjects joined as one subject and two verbs, but it still contains one independent clause.

### Compound sentence: two or more independent clauses

A **compound sentence** has at least two independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction or a semicolon.

> **The schedule begins Monday, and the team meets Tuesday.**
>
> **The schedule begins Monday; the team meets Tuesday.**

Both clauses can stand alone. When a comma and a coordinating conjunction join independent clauses, place the comma before the conjunction. Chapter 14 covers punctuation in more detail.

### Complex sentence: one independent clause and at least one dependent clause

A **complex sentence** contains one independent clause and at least one dependent clause.

> **Because the schedule begins Monday**, the team meets Friday.
>
> The team meets Friday **because the schedule begins Monday**.

Subordination shows that one idea has a supporting relationship to another, such as a reason, time, condition, or contrast. Use a comma after an introductory dependent clause; a following dependent clause usually does not need a comma.

### Compound-complex sentence: multiple independent clauses plus a dependent clause

A **compound-complex sentence** has at least two independent clauses and at least one dependent clause.

> **Because the schedule begins Monday**, the team meets Friday, **and the director sends an update**.

The dependent clause is *because the schedule begins Monday*. The two independent clauses are *the team meets Friday* and *the director sends an update*.

This structure can show several relationships at once, but a long compound-complex sentence may be difficult to follow. Use it only when the connections are clear.

### Structure and purpose are different

The sentence *Can you send the revised schedule when you have a moment?* is interrogative by purpose and complex by structure. The sentence *Please send the revised schedule when you have a moment.* is imperative by purpose and complex by structure. Identify purpose by what the sentence does; identify structure by its clauses.

## 13.5 Coordination and subordination

Writers combine ideas to show how they relate. Two important methods are **coordination** and **subordination**.

### Coordination: ideas at a similar level

Coordination joins ideas that have equal or similar importance. Use a coordinating conjunction such as *and, but, or,* or *so*, or use a semicolon between closely related independent clauses:

> The deadline moved, **so** we revised the work plan.
>
> The deadline moved; **we revised the work plan**.

Coordination is useful when both ideas deserve similar emphasis. Avoid joining unrelated ideas merely because they are both true.

### Subordination: one idea supports another

Subordination presents one idea as background, a reason, a condition, a time reference, or a contrast. A subordinating conjunction introduces the dependent clause:

> **Because the deadline moved**, we revised the work plan. (Reason.)
>
> **When the deadline moves**, update the work plan. (Time.)
>
> **Although the deadline moved**, the team finished on time. (Contrast.)

The dependent clause cannot stand alone as a complete sentence in these examples. Subordination is useful when one idea is more important and another provides supporting information.

### Choose a relationship, not just a connector

Connectors signal logic. Compare:

> The file was corrupted, **so** we restored the backup. (The corruption led to the restoration.)
>
> **Although** the file was corrupted, we restored the backup. (Contrast: despite the corruption, restoration was possible.)

Both sentences are grammatical, but they make different claims. Choose the structure that matches what happened.

## 13.6 Fragments, run-ons, and comma splices

Sentence boundaries show where one complete thought ends and another begins. Three common problems are fragments, run-ons, and comma splices.

### Fragments

A **fragment** is a word group punctuated as if it were a complete sentence but missing an independent clause or an essential part of one.

> Fragment: **Although the meeting ended early.**
>
> Complete: **Although the meeting ended early, we finished the agenda.**

> Fragment: **The updated file in the shared folder.**
>
> Complete: **The updated file in the shared folder is ready.**

To repair a fragment, attach it to a related independent clause, or add the missing subject or verb. A phrase or dependent clause may be used intentionally as a fragment in informal or creative writing, but formal sentences generally require a complete independent clause.

### Run-ons

A **run-on sentence** incorrectly joins two independent clauses without suitable punctuation or a conjunction. A **fused sentence** is a run-on with no punctuation between the clauses:

> Fused: **The call ended we sent the notes.**

Repair a run-on by choosing one of several methods:

1. **Use a period:** The call ended. We sent the notes.
2. **Use a comma and coordinating conjunction:** The call ended, **so** we sent the notes.
3. **Use a semicolon:** The call ended; we sent the notes.
4. **Make one idea dependent:** **After the call ended**, we sent the notes.

Choose the repair that best reflects the relationship between the ideas. A semicolon works best when the clauses are closely related.

### Comma splices

A **comma splice** joins two independent clauses with only a comma:

> Comma splice: **The call ended, we sent the notes.**

Repair it with a period, semicolon, coordinating conjunction, or subordination:

> The call ended. We sent the notes.
>
> The call ended, **so** we sent the notes.
>
> **After the call ended**, we sent the notes.

A comma alone is not strong enough to join two independent clauses in standard edited prose. Chapter 14 explains punctuation choices in depth.

## 13.7 Sentence variety, emphasis, and concision

Clear writing uses a mix of sentence structures and lengths. Variety keeps a passage from sounding mechanical, while a clear pattern helps readers follow the main point.

### Vary sentence structure

Compare a series of simple sentences:

> The lab was busy. The team needed more space. The manager reserved a second room.

The ideas can be combined to show a reason and a result:

> **Because the lab was busy, the manager reserved a second room for the team.**

Or coordinate two actions that carry similar weight:

> **The lab was busy, so the manager reserved a second room.**

The best choice depends on what you want to emphasize. Do not combine every short sentence; short sentences can add clarity or emphasis.

### Use sentence openings for emphasis

The opening of a sentence tells readers what to focus on first:

> **After the final review**, the team submitted the report.
>
> **The team** submitted the report after the final review.

The first version emphasizes when; the second emphasizes who. Choose the opening that best connects with the previous sentence or highlights the main point.

### Keep sentences concise

Concision means expressing the necessary meaning with no unnecessary words. It does not mean making every sentence as short as possible.

> Wordy: Due to the fact that the room was unavailable, we made a decision to meet online.
>
> Concise: Because the room was unavailable, we decided to meet online.

Remove repeated ideas, vague openings, and phrases that do not add meaning. Keep information the reader needs to understand the point.

### Keep the main subject and verb easy to find

Too many interruptions between the subject and verb can make a sentence hard to follow:

> Crowded: The workshop, which the department that supports new employees organized after several schedule changes, was useful.
>
> Clearer: The department organized the workshop after several schedule changes. The workshop helped new employees.

Long sentences can be effective when their structure is clear. If readers must hold too many details in mind before reaching the main verb, divide the sentence or move some information to another sentence.

## 13.8 Common sentence problems

### A sentence that is long but not complex

Sentence length does not determine structure. A simple sentence can be long if it contains phrases; a short sentence can be complex:

> Long simple: The students in the evening program near the north entrance waited after class.
>
> Short complex: We left when it ended.

### A sentence with too many *and*s

Repeated *and* can make a list of events hard to follow. Use punctuation, a time phrase, or a new sentence to organize the sequence:

> Crowded: I opened the file and checked the numbers and wrote a summary and sent it to the team.
>
> Clearer: I opened the file and checked the numbers. Then I wrote a summary and sent it to the team.

### A fragment after a period

Words such as *because, although, if,* and *when* often introduce dependent clauses. Check whether the clause has a complete main clause attached.

### A comma between clauses without a connector

If both sides of a comma can stand as complete sentences, use a conjunction, semicolon, or period rather than the comma alone.

## 13.9 Practice

### A. Combine and revise short sentences

Combine each set as directed. Make the relationship clear and punctuate the sentence correctly. More than one answer may work.

1. The office was closed. We left the documents with security. (Use *so*.)
2. The instructions were brief. They were easy to follow. (Use *but* to show an unexpected contrast.)
3. The interview ends. We will compare our notes. (Use a time clause beginning with *after*.)
4. The report is complete. The charts still need labels. (Create a compound sentence.)
5. The bus was late. I arrived before the presentation began. (Use *although*.)
6. The team reviewed the proposal. The team corrected two figures. The team sent it to the director. (Combine without making the sentence difficult to follow.)

### B. Diagnose and repair

Label each item as a complete sentence, fragment, fused run-on, or comma splice. Repair each fragment or run-on in a suitable way.

1. Because the building closes at six.
2. The building closes at six, so we should leave soon.
3. The building closes at six we should leave soon.
4. The building closes at six, we should leave soon.
5. After the meeting, the director sent a summary.
6. Although the survey received many responses, the research team needs more time.

### C. Edit the message for flow

Revise the message to correct sentence boundaries, improve the flow, and remove unnecessary repetition. There are at least four issues.

> Hi everyone,
>
> The new room is available. Because the original room needs repairs. We can meet there tomorrow, the room has a screen and video equipment. I checked it and tested the screen and I tested the audio and everything works. If you arrive before 9:00.
>
> Thanks,
> Lee

### D. Build a paragraph with varied structures

Write a paragraph of five to seven sentences about a class, workplace task, or event. Include:

- one simple sentence;
- one compound sentence;
- one complex sentence;
- one sentence that begins with a dependent clause; and
- at least one short sentence for emphasis or clarity.

Underline each independent clause once and each dependent clause twice. Then revise one sentence for concision and one for emphasis.

## 13.10 Answer key

### A. Combine and revise short sentences

Answers may vary. Sample answers:

1. **The office was closed, so we left the documents with security.** The second clause is a result.
2. **The instructions were brief, but they were easy to follow.** *But* expresses an unexpected contrast.
3. **After the interview ends, we will compare our notes.** The introductory dependent time clause is followed by a comma.
4. **The report is complete, but the charts still need labels.** A comma and coordinator join two independent clauses.
5. **Although the bus was late, I arrived before the presentation began.** The dependent clause contrasts with the result.
6. **The team reviewed the proposal, corrected two figures, and sent it to the director.** The shared subject makes a compound predicate; no second independent clause is needed.

### B. Diagnose and repair

1. **Fragment.** Repair: **Because the building closes at six, we should leave soon.**
2. **Complete compound sentence.** The comma and *so* correctly join two independent clauses.
3. **Fused run-on.** Repair: **The building closes at six, so we should leave soon.** Other options include a period, semicolon, or dependent clause.
4. **Comma splice.** Repair: **The building closes at six; we should leave soon.** Or: **The building closes at six, so we should leave soon.**
5. **Complete sentence.** It has one independent clause; *after the meeting* is a phrase, not a dependent clause.
6. **Complete complex sentence.** The first clause is dependent; the second is independent.

### C. Edit the message for flow

Answers will vary. One possible revision is:

> Hi everyone,
>
> The new room is available because the original room needs repairs. We can meet there tomorrow. The room has a screen and video equipment, and I tested both; everything works. Please arrive before 9:00.
>
> Thanks,
> Lee

This revision attaches the *because* clause to a complete sentence, repairs the comma splice, combines the repeated equipment checks, removes the fragment *If you arrive before 9:00*, and turns the intended arrival time into a clear request. A shorter alternative is **The room's screen and audio equipment both work.**

### D. Build a paragraph with varied structures

Answers will vary. Check that each sentence contains at least one independent clause, that dependent clauses are attached to independent clauses, and that the paragraph uses a mix of structures. The short sentence should add emphasis or clarity rather than interrupting the flow without purpose.

## Chapter summary

A complete sentence in standard writing is built around at least one independent clause, begins with a capital letter, and ends with appropriate punctuation. The subject tells who or what the sentence is about; the predicate says something about it. Objects receive actions or complete verbs, while complements complete or describe subjects and objects. Sentences may be declarative, interrogative, imperative, or exclamatory in purpose, and simple, compound, complex, or compound-complex in structure. Coordination joins ideas of similar importance; subordination makes one idea support another. Repair fragments by completing or attaching them, and repair run-ons or comma splices with a period, semicolon, conjunction, or dependent clause. Vary sentence openings and lengths to guide emphasis, but keep the main meaning easy to follow and remove words that do not help the reader.
