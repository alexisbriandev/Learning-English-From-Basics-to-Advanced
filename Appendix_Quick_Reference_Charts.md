# Appendix. Quick Reference Charts

Use these charts to review a pattern while reading or editing. They are reminders, not complete rule books: context and meaning still matter. For explanations and practice, see the chapter listed in each section.

## A. Parts of speech

A word's **part of speech** is the job it performs in a particular sentence. The same spelling can do different jobs in different contexts. See Chapter 1 and the focused chapters below.

| Word class | Basic job | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Noun** | Names a person, place, thing, idea, or experience | The **student** revised an **essay**. |
| **Pronoun** | Stands in for a noun or noun phrase | **She** revised it. |
| **Adjective** | Describes or limits a noun or pronoun | The **careful** student revised it. |
| **Verb** | Expresses an action, event, state, or relationship | The student **revised** the essay. |
| **Adverb** | Modifies a verb, adjective, adverb, or whole clause | She revised it **quickly**. |
| **Preposition** | Shows a relationship; begins a prepositional phrase | She revised it **after** class. |
| **Conjunction** | Connects words, phrases, or clauses | She revised it, **and** she submitted it. |
| **Determiner** | Comes before a noun to identify or limit it; articles are one kind | **The** student revised **her** essay. |
| **Interjection** | Expresses a reaction or manages interaction | **Well**, the draft is ready. |

**Quick identification questions:** What does the word name or replace? What does it modify? What relationship or connection does it signal? What role does it play in this sentence?

## B. Verb forms

Verb patterns combine a main verb with any helping verbs. Regular verbs often form the past and past participle with *-ed*; irregular verbs have forms that must be learned. See Chapter 5.

| Form | Common use | Regular example: *revise* | Irregular example: *write* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Base** | After *to*, modals, and *do/does/did*; present with *I/you/we/they* | revise | write |
| **Third-person singular present** | Simple present with *he/she/it* or a singular noun | revises | writes |
| **Past** | Completed past action | revised | wrote |
| **Past participle** | With *have/has/had*; also used in many passive forms | revised | written |
| **-ing form** | With a form of *be* for progressive aspect; also used in other structures | revising | writing |

### Common time and aspect patterns

| Pattern | Form | Example | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple present | Base; add *-s/-es* for third-person singular | She **works** on campus. | Habits, facts, schedules |
| Simple past | Past form | She **worked** yesterday. | Completed past events |
| Future meaning | *will* + base; also *be going to* + base | She **will work** tomorrow. | Prediction, decision, plan, or intention (choice depends on context) |
| Present progressive | *am/is/are* + *-ing* | She **is working** now. | Action in progress or a temporary situation |
| Past progressive | *was/were* + *-ing* | She **was working** at noon. | Action in progress at a past time |
| Present perfect | *have/has* + past participle | She **has worked** here for a year. | Past action or state connected to the present |
| Past perfect | *had* + past participle | She **had worked** there before she moved. | Earlier of two past events |
| Passive | Form of *be* + past participle | The report **was reviewed**. | Emphasizes the action or receiver; name the doer when useful |

**Modal pattern:** modal + base verb: *can finish, might need, should ask, will send*. Do not add *-s* to the main verb after a modal: *She can finish*, not *She can finishes*.

## C. Sentence patterns

An independent clause can stand as a sentence. A dependent clause cannot stand alone in standard edited writing. See Chapters 12 and 13.

| Pattern | Basic structure | Example | Useful when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | One independent clause | The team reviewed the schedule. | Stating one main idea directly |
| **Compound** | Two or more independent clauses joined correctly | The team reviewed the schedule, and the manager approved it. | Connecting ideas of similar importance |
| **Complex** | One independent clause plus at least one dependent clause | After the team reviewed the schedule, the manager approved it. | Showing a relationship such as time, cause, condition, or contrast |
| **Compound-complex** | Two or more independent clauses and at least one dependent clause | After the team reviewed the schedule, the manager approved it, and staff sent an update. | Connecting several related ideas and showing their relationships |

**Sentence-boundary check:** Do not join two independent clauses with only a comma. Use a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a coordinating conjunction (*and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet*), as appropriate.

## D. Agreement reminders

Agreement means that grammatical forms that belong together match. To check a verb, identify the true subject. To check a pronoun, identify its antecedent and the pronoun's role. See Chapters 3 and 11.

| Situation | Reminder | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Singular subject | Use a singular verb in the present tense | The **list is** ready. |
| Plural subject | Use a plural verb in the present tense | The **lists are** ready. |
| Intervening phrase | Do not let a nearby noun distract you from the subject | The **list** of names **is** ready. |
| *Each/every* | Usually singular when each person or item is considered individually | **Each** applicant **has** a reference. |
| Compound subject with *and* | Usually plural | Mina **and** Jo **are** presenting. |
| *Or/nor* subjects | In many cases, the verb agrees with the nearer subject; rewrite if the result sounds awkward | Neither the interns nor the **manager is** available. |
| Collective noun | Choose agreement that fits the variety and whether the group is treated as a unit or individuals; stay consistent | The **team is** ready. |
| *There is/are* | The following noun phrase indicates whether singular or plural agreement is needed | **There are** two changes. |
| Pronoun and antecedent | Keep number/person clear; singular *they* is standard when referring to one person whose pronouns are *they/them* or when gender is unknown or irrelevant | Every student should bring **their** ID. |

**Meaning can matter:** With expressions of amount, collective nouns, or *data*, agreement may depend on whether the writer presents the group as a unit, its members, or individual items. In academic and professional writing, follow the required style and use a consistent convention.

## E. Article and determiner choices

Articles are determiners. Before choosing an article, ask whether the noun is count or noncount, singular or plural, and specific or general. See Chapters 2 and 9.

| Choice | Common use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **a/an** | One nonspecific singular count noun; choose by the first sound, not the first letter | **a** report; **an** email; **a** university; **an** hour |
| **the** | A specific or identifiable person/thing; something already mentioned or shared in context | I read **a** report. **The** report was clear. |
| **No article (zero article)** | Often used with plural or noncount nouns in general statements | **Students** need **information**. |
| **No article in many fixed contexts** | Common with some uses of meals, school, work, and place names; meaning and local usage matter | We had lunch. She is at work. |
| **this/that/these/those** | Identifies by distance or context; match singular/plural | **This** form; **those** forms |
| **my/your/our/their** | Shows a relationship or possession before a noun | **Their** schedule changed. |
| **some/any** | Quantity; choice often depends on sentence type and intended meaning, with exceptions | We need **some** time. Do you have **any** questions? |
| **many/few** | Used with plural count nouns | **Many** applicants; **few** openings |
| **much/little** | Used with noncount nouns | **Much** work; **little** time |
| **each/every** | Refers to members individually; followed by a singular count noun | **Each student**; **every meeting** |

**Remember:** A singular count noun usually needs a determiner: *a schedule, the schedule, my schedule*. Noncount nouns such as *advice* and *information* usually do not take *a/an*: *some advice, a piece of information*.

## F. Punctuation checks

Punctuation helps readers see sentence structure and meaning; it is not simply a record of pauses in speech. These are quick checks for common situations. See Chapter 14 for fuller guidance.

| Mark | Check | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Period (.)** | End a statement or a polite command | The report is ready. Please send it today. |
| **Question mark (?)** | End a direct question | When is the meeting? |
| **Exclamation point (!)** | Use sparingly for strong feeling or emphasis | We finished the project! |
| **Comma (,)** | Separate items in a series; follow an introductory phrase or clause; join independent clauses with a coordinating conjunction; set off nonessential information | We checked the date, time, and room. **After lunch,** we met. The room was ready, **but** the projector was not. |
| **Semicolon (;)** | Join closely related independent clauses without a coordinating conjunction; separate complex list items when needed | The draft is complete; the review begins tomorrow. |
| **Colon (:)** | Introduce an explanation, example, or list after a complete clause | Bring three items: your ID, the form, and a pen. |
| **Apostrophe (’)** | Mark a contraction or possession; do not use it to make an ordinary plural | **it's** = *it is*; **the student's notes**; **three students** |
| **Quotation marks (“ ”)** | Mark direct speech or quoted words; use a clear attribution when needed | “I sent the file,” she said. |
| **Parentheses ( )** | Enclose supplementary information that is not central to the sentence | The session is online (the link is in the calendar invitation). |
| **Hyphen (-)** | Join some compound modifiers before a noun when needed for clarity; follow the chosen style for variable compounds | a **well-written** summary |
| **Dash (—)** | Mark a break or emphasis; use consistently and avoid overuse | The result was clear—students needed more time. |

### Fast punctuation and capitalization pass

- Does every sentence begin with a capital letter and end with appropriate punctuation?
- Are independent clauses joined correctly?
- Are commas marking a clear structure rather than separating a subject from its verb?
- Are apostrophes used for contractions or possession, not ordinary plurals?
- Are names, places, languages, days, and months capitalized?
- Are quotation marks and punctuation placed consistently according to the document's style?
- Does the punctuation support the meaning you intend?
