# Chapter 14. Punctuation and Capitalization

## What you will learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- use punctuation to show sentence structure and meaning;
- choose appropriate end marks and use commas, semicolons, and colons in common patterns;
- use apostrophes for contractions and possession, not ordinary plurals;
- punctuate dialogue and quotations consistently;
- use parentheses, hyphens, and dashes for their main purposes; and
- capitalize sentence beginnings, names, titles, languages, dates, and headings consistently.

## 14.1 Punctuation guides readers

Punctuation helps readers see how words and ideas fit together. It marks sentence boundaries, separates items, shows relationships between clauses, and distinguishes a writer's words from quoted words. It is not simply a written version of pauses in speech.

Compare:

> **Let's review, team.** (The writer is addressing the team.)
>
> **Let's review the team.** (The sentence now suggests reviewing the team.)

A comma changes the meaning. Good punctuation helps the reader interpret the sentence you intend.

This chapter uses a consistent house style based on the **Chicago Manual of Style, 17th edition**, where applicable. For example, it uses the serial comma in a list and places periods and commas inside closing quotation marks. Other publishers and English-language varieties may use different conventions; follow the style required for your course or workplace.

## 14.2 Periods, question marks, and exclamation points

### Periods

Use a **period** at the end of a declarative sentence and most imperative sentences:

> The seminar begins at 10:00.
>
> Please save a copy of the file.

Use one space after a period in standard digital writing. Do not add a period after a heading or title unless the format calls for one.

### Question marks

Use a **question mark** after a direct question:

> When does the seminar begin?
>
> Could you send the revised file?

An indirect question is usually part of a statement and takes a period:

> I wonder when the seminar begins.

### Exclamation points

Use an **exclamation point** for a strong reaction, urgent warning, or emphatic instruction:

> Watch out!
>
> We finished the project!

Exclamation points can make a message sound excited, angry, or forceful. Use them selectively in academic and workplace writing. One is usually enough.

### Avoid using punctuation to replace sentence structure

A period cannot turn a fragment into a complete sentence, and a comma cannot join two independent clauses by itself. Check that the sentence is structurally complete before choosing an end mark. Chapters 12 and 13 explain phrases, clauses, and sentence boundaries.

## 14.3 Commas

Commas separate parts of a sentence so readers can follow the structure. Use them for a clear reason; do not place them wherever you would pause while speaking.

### Items in a series

Use commas between three or more items in a series. Chicago style includes a comma before the final conjunction; this is called the **serial comma**:

> Please bring a notebook, a pen, **and** your student card.
>
> The program serves students, researchers, **and** local organizations.

The serial comma helps prevent ambiguity when list items are complex or contain conjunctions.

### After an introductory word, phrase, or clause

Use a comma after an introductory dependent clause:

> **When the meeting ends,** please turn off the projector.
>
> **Because the room was unavailable,** we met online.

A comma often follows an introductory phrase, especially if it is long or if the comma helps prevent misreading:

> **After the final review,** the team submitted the report.
>
> **In the afternoon,** we will meet with the client.

Short introductory phrases may not always need a comma, but use one when it helps the reader.

### Before a coordinating conjunction joining independent clauses

Use a comma before *and, but, or, nor, for, so,* or *yet* when it joins two independent clauses:

> The printer stopped working, **so** we called technical support.

Both sides can stand as sentences: *The printer stopped working.* / *We called technical support.*

Do not normally add a comma when the conjunction joins only words, phrases, or two verbs with the same subject:

> I packed a charger **and** a notebook.
>
> The assistant checked the dates **and** confirmed the room.

### Around nonessential information

Use commas around a **nonessential** element—information that can be removed without changing which person or thing the sentence identifies:

> Our new office, **which opened in May,** has two meeting rooms.
>
> Dr. Chen, **the program director,** will welcome the guests.

Do not use commas around information needed to identify the noun:

> The office **that opened in May** has two meeting rooms.
>
> Employees **who work remotely** can join online.

The relative-clause commas can change the meaning. Chapter 12 explains restrictive and nonrestrictive clauses in more detail.

### Common comma errors

Do not separate a subject from its verb or a verb from its object with a comma:

> Incorrect: The list of materials, **is** attached.
>
> Correct: The list of materials **is** attached.
>
> Incorrect: We reviewed, **the schedule**.
>
> Correct: We reviewed **the schedule**.

Do not join two independent clauses with only a comma. That creates a **comma splice**:

> Comma splice: The room was unavailable, we met online.
>
> Correct: The room was unavailable, **so** we met online.

You can also use a period, semicolon, or subordination. See Chapter 13.

## 14.4 Semicolons

A **semicolon** is stronger than a comma and weaker than a period. Use it mainly to join closely related independent clauses when no coordinating conjunction appears:

> The first room was unavailable; we met in the library instead.

Both parts must be independent clauses. A semicolon does not replace a comma before a conjunction:

> Correct: The first room was unavailable, **so** we met in the library.
>
> Incorrect: The first room was unavailable; **so** we met in the library.

Use semicolons to separate items in a series when the items already contain commas:

> The panel included Dr. Lin, a biologist; Professor Ahmed, an economist; and Mei Chen, a student representative.

Do not use a semicolon simply because a sentence feels long. A period may be clearer.

## 14.5 Colons

A **colon** introduces an explanation, example, quotation, or list. The words before the colon should usually form a complete clause.

> The team needs three items: a projector, a screen, and an adapter.
>
> She made one request: Please send the final draft by noon.

Do not place a colon directly after a verb or preposition when the sentence continues as a list:

> Incorrect: The team needs: a projector, a screen, and an adapter.
>
> Correct: The team needs a projector, a screen, and an adapter.

In this book's Chicago 17 house style, the first word after a colon within a sentence is usually lowercase unless it is a proper name. Capitalize it when the colon introduces direct speech or a quotation, a direct question, or two or more complete sentences:

> The checklist has one final step: **save** a copy of the file.
>
> The presenter asked: “**Where** should we meet?”
>
> She had two concerns: **The** room was too small. **The** projector was not working.

Some current style guides capitalize a complete sentence after a colon in more situations. Follow the requested style consistently.

## 14.6 Apostrophes

An **apostrophe** marks a contraction or possession. It does not normally make a noun plural.

### Contractions

A contraction combines words and uses an apostrophe to show omitted letters:

| Full form | Contraction |
|---|---|
| *do not* | *don't* |
| *is not* | *isn't* |
| *we are* | *we're* |
| *they have* | *they've* |
| *it is / it has* | *it's* |

Contractions are common in conversation and informal messages. In formal academic writing, the writer may prefer the full forms, but consistency and the assignment's style matter.

### Possessive nouns

Use an apostrophe to show ownership or another relationship:

- Singular noun: add *'s* — **the student's** notebook; **the manager's** office.
- Plural noun ending in *-s*: add an apostrophe — **the students'** projects; **the managers'** offices.
- Irregular plural not ending in *-s*: add *'s* — **the children's** books; **the people's** choice.
- Singular name ending in *-s*: in this Chicago-based house style, generally add *'s* — **James's** report.

For joint possession, add a possessive ending to the final name when the owners share one item: **Maya and Luis's office**. If each person has a separate item, make both names possessive: **Maya's and Luis's offices**.

### Possessives are not plurals

> **The student's** question was clear. (One student; possession or association.)
>
> **The students'** questions were clear. (Multiple students; possession or association.)
>
> **The students** asked questions. (Plural noun; no possession, so no apostrophe.)

### Possessive pronouns and commonly confused forms

Possessive pronouns do not use apostrophes: *yours, hers, ours,* and *theirs*.

- *its* = belonging to it; *it's* = *it is* or *it has*;
- *whose* = belonging to whom; *who's* = *who is* or *who has*;
- *your* = belonging to you; *you're* = *you are*;
- *their* = belonging to them; *they're* = *they are*.

> The company changed **its** hours. **It's** now open on Saturdays.

## 14.7 Quotation marks and attribution

Use double quotation marks for direct speech and short quotations in this book's Chicago-based style. Use single quotation marks for a quotation within another quotation.

### Direct speech

Use a comma to introduce a direct quotation after a speech tag such as *said* or *asked*:

> Maya said, “The meeting starts at ten.”
>
> “The meeting starts at ten,” Maya said.

If the quoted speech is a question or exclamation, keep its mark inside the quotation marks:

> “**When** does the meeting start?” Maya asked.
>
> “**Watch out!**” Omar shouted.

Do not add a comma after a question mark or exclamation point before a speech tag:

> Correct: “Are we ready?” she asked.
>
> Incorrect: “Are we ready?,” she asked.

### Quoted words within a sentence

Use quotation marks for a short direct quotation. In Chicago style, commas and periods normally go inside the closing quotation marks:

> The report calls the change “a practical improvement.”
>
> She described the proposal as “clear,” “realistic,” and “affordable.”

Question marks and exclamation points go inside or outside depending on whether they belong to the quoted words or to the whole sentence:

> Did the report use the phrase “a practical improvement”? (The whole sentence is the question.)
>
> She asked, “Is this the final version?” (The quoted words are a question.)

Colons and semicolons normally go outside closing quotation marks unless they are part of the quoted material:

> The report describes the change as “a practical improvement”; the team plans to test it.

### Attribution and quotation accuracy

Attribution tells readers who said or wrote the quoted words. Introduce a quotation only when the source is clear:

> The project lead wrote, “Please use the revised schedule.”

Do not change a person's words without marking the change or explaining it. For a short excerpt, preserve the original meaning and punctuation as appropriate. If a quotation needs so much explanation that it interrupts the sentence, paraphrase it and name the source instead.

### Quotation within a quotation

Use single quotation marks for a quote inside a quote:

> “The instructor said, ‘Submit the form by noon,’ and then ended the session,” Lee recalled.

## 14.8 Parentheses

**Parentheses** enclose extra information that is useful but not essential to the main sentence:

> The office will reopen on Monday (after the maintenance work is complete).

If the parenthetical material is part of a larger sentence, place the sentence's final punctuation outside the closing parenthesis:

> We will meet in the library (Room 204) after lunch.

If the parentheses contain a complete sentence, place its ending punctuation inside:

> The office will reopen on Monday. (The maintenance work should finish by Sunday.)

Use parentheses sparingly. If the added information is essential, put it in the main sentence instead.

## 14.9 Hyphens and dashes

Hyphens and dashes are different marks with different jobs.

### Hyphens

A **hyphen** joins parts of some compound words, especially when they work together as an adjective before a noun:

> a **part-time** position
>
> a **well-known** researcher
>
> a **two-week** course

Many compounds are open (*student services*), closed (*workplace*), or hyphenated (*part-time*). Usage can change over time, so check a current dictionary or the required style guide when uncertain. A compound modifier after the noun is often open: *The position is part time.*

Do not usually hyphenate a compound modifier when its first word is an *-ly* adverb:

> a **highly skilled** technician

### En dashes

An **en dash** (–) can connect a range of numbers, dates, or times:

> pages **12–18**
>
> **9:00 a.m.–12:00 p.m.**

For short learner writing, *from 9:00 a.m. to noon* is also clear. Do not use an en dash to replace *to* in a construction that already begins with *from*: write *from 9 to 12*, not *from 9–12*.

### Em dashes

An **em dash** (—) marks a strong interruption, an abrupt change, or an emphasized aside:

> The first draft—although complete—needs a clearer conclusion.
>
> We had one option left—reschedule the interview.

Chicago style uses closed em dashes, with no spaces on either side. A pair of commas or parentheses may be quieter alternatives. Use dashes selectively; too many can make writing feel jumpy.

## 14.10 Capitalization

Capitalization signals sentence beginnings, names, and titles. Capitalize consistently and preserve the official spelling of names.

### Sentence beginnings and direct speech

Capitalize the first word of a sentence and the first word of a complete direct quotation:

> **The** review is complete.
>
> She said, “**The** review is complete.”

After a colon in a running sentence, follow the book's Chicago 17 rule described in section 14.5.

### Names and places

Capitalize proper nouns: specific names of people, organizations, buildings, cities, countries, and geographic features.

> **Dr. Mei Chen** works at **East Harbor University** in **Taipei**.

Do not capitalize a general noun unless it is part of an official name:

> The **university** is near the **harbor**.
>
> **East Harbor University** is near the **Pacific Ocean**.

### Titles and positions

Capitalize a formal title when it immediately precedes a person's name:

> **Professor Lin** will lead the discussion.
>
> **Director Patel** approved the budget.

Lowercase a title when it follows a name or is used generally:

> Lin, **a professor** of biology, will lead the discussion.
>
> The **director** approved the budget.

Official titles may have organization-specific capitalization. Preserve them when required, but do not capitalize every job title for emphasis.

### Languages, nationalities, days, and months

Capitalize names of languages and nationalities, days of the week, months, and holidays:

> She speaks **English** and **Mandarin**.
>
> We will meet on **Thursday, October 22**.
>
> The office is closed on **Lunar New Year**.

Names of seasons are usually lowercase: *spring, summer, autumn/fall,* and *winter*.

### Academic subjects

Capitalize the name of a language or a proper name, but usually lowercase general academic subjects:

> He studies **English** and **biology**.
>
> The course covers **Asian history** and **statistics**.

Course titles and official department names may use capitals: *Introduction to Biology; Department of Statistics*.

### Headings and titles

Styles differ in how they capitalize titles and headings. This book uses **Chicago headline style** for chapter titles: capitalize the first and last words and the major words (such as nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs); lowercase articles, coordinating conjunctions, prepositions, and the infinitive *to* unless they are the first or last word. Under the 17th edition, prepositions are lowercased regardless of length in title case. Other publishers may use sentence case or a different title-case rule, so follow the requested style and use it consistently.

## 14.11 A practical editing checklist

When you edit punctuation and capitalization, work from the largest units to the smallest:

1. **Sentence boundaries:** Does each sentence contain a complete thought? Are fragments and run-ons repaired?
2. **Clause relationships:** Are commas, semicolons, and conjunctions showing how the clauses connect?
3. **Lists and extra information:** Are series, introductions, and nonessential clauses punctuated clearly?
4. **Possession and quotation:** Do apostrophes, quotation marks, and attributions show the intended meaning?
5. **Names and headings:** Are proper nouns and titles capitalized consistently?
6. **Consistency:** Does the document follow one style for serial commas, quotation marks, headings, and dashes?

## 14.13 Answer key

### A. Punctuate the dialogue

> **Maya asked, “Did you send the final report?”**
>
> **“No, I'll send it after lunch,” Omar replied.**
>
> **“Please include the appendix,” the manager said.**
>
> **“I thought you had already sent it,” Maya said.**

The question mark belongs inside the quotation marks because the quoted words form a question. The other direct quotations are statements or requests, so their commas and periods follow the standard Chicago placement.

### B. Correct the announcement

> **Notice: Student Research Forum**
>
> The forum will take place on **Thursday, April 23, at 2:00 p.m. in West Hall.** Please bring your ID, notebook, and a copy of your abstract.
>
> The coordinator said, “Please arrive by 1:45.”
>
> Questions? Contact Maya Chen at Student Services or email **forum@eastharbor.edu**.

Capitalized the heading, weekday, month, place name, and personal and organizational names; divided the first line into complete sentences; added commas to the list and introductory speech tag; and used standard end punctuation. Depending on the institution's official naming, *West Hall* and *Student Services* may have different capitalization; preserve the official forms.

### C. Make a capitalization pass

> **Planning a Visit to East Harbor University**
>
> On **Monday, June 8**, **Professor Mei Chen** will welcome the visitors from **Singapore**. The group will meet at the **Student Center** before touring the **Department of Biology**. After lunch, they will attend a short session about **English** language learning and **Taiwanese** history. **Director Lin** will answer questions in **West Hall**.

The heading follows Chicago headline style. Capitalize the day, month, proper place and institution names, formal titles used before names, language and nationality names, and official building or department names. If *student center* or *department of biology* is not an official name in the writer's context, lowercase it; capitalize official names consistently.

### D. Check punctuation choices

1. **Colon** — It introduces the goals after a complete clause: *The training has two goals.*
2. **, and** — The comma is part of Chicago's serial-comma style for a list of three items.
3. **;** — Both sides are independent clauses, and the semicolon joins closely related ideas. A comma plus *but* would also work: *The survey is short, but it still asks useful questions.*
4. **“When does the workshop begin?” the visitor asked.** — The quoted words are a direct question, so the question mark belongs inside the quotation marks. No comma follows the question mark before the speech tag.
5. **students'** — The plural noun ends in *-s*, so the possessive apostrophe follows the *s*.
6. **students** — The noun is plural, not possessive; no apostrophe is needed.

### E. Apply it

Answers will vary. Check that the sentences are complete, the list uses consistent commas, the quotation is attributed, the possessive apostrophe has the right placement, and proper names are capitalized accurately.

## Chapter summary

Punctuation guides readers through sentence boundaries, lists, clause relationships, quotations, and extra information. Use periods, question marks, and exclamation points according to sentence purpose. Use commas for lists, introductory elements, independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction, and nonessential information; do not separate a subject from its verb. Semicolons join closely related independent clauses or separate complex list items. Colons introduce lists, explanations, or quotations after a complete clause. Apostrophes mark contractions and possession, not ordinary plurals. Quotation marks and attribution show who said what. Parentheses set off secondary information; hyphens join some compounds, while en and em dashes have different uses. Capitalize sentence beginnings and proper names, and follow one title and heading style consistently. This chapter follows Chicago 17 in applicable choices, including the serial comma and its title-capitalization conventions.
