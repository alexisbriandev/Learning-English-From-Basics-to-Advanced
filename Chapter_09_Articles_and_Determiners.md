# Chapter 9. Articles and Determiners

## What you will learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- explain what determiners do and recognize articles as one type of determiner;
- choose among *a, an, the,* and no article;
- use first and later mention, shared or specific reference, and general statements to guide article choices;
- use demonstrative, possessive, and quantity determiners;
- match determiners to count and noncount nouns; and
- apply common article patterns with sounds, places, meals, school, and work.

## 9.1 What is a determiner?

A **determiner** is a word that comes before a noun to identify it or limit its meaning. It helps readers know which person or thing you mean, whose it is, or how many or how much you mean.

> **the** schedule
>
> **my** desk
>
> **several** questions

The noun is the main word; the determiner comes before it. A noun phrase can include more than one kind of description:

> **Those two useful books** are on **the** table.

*Those* and *two* identify or limit *books*; *useful* is an adjective describing them. *The* identifies which table. In this book, words such as *this, my,* and *several* are determiners when they come before nouns. Some grammar books classify certain determiners differently.

### Articles are one kind of determiner

English has three article choices:

- **the** — the definite article;
- **a** and **an** — the indefinite articles; and
- **zero article** — no article is used before the noun.

> **The** room is ready. (A particular room.)
>
> We need **a** room. (Any one room; not yet identified.)
>
> **Rooms** are available. (Plural noun used generally; no article.)

The choice depends on what the speaker and reader know, whether the noun is count or noncount, and whether the sentence refers to something specific or general.

## 9.2 A practical article decision process

When you need an article, ask these questions in order:

1. **Is there already another determiner before the noun?** If yes, do not normally add an article. Use *my report*, not *the my report*; use *those files*, not *the those files*.
2. **Is the noun singular and countable?** If so, it usually needs a determiner. Choose *a/an* or *the* (or another determiner such as *my* or *this*).
3. **Does the reader know which one you mean?** Use *the* when it is identifiable from context, shared knowledge, or a previous mention.
4. **Are you introducing one nonspecific countable thing?** Use *a* or *an*.
5. **Are you speaking generally about plural count nouns or noncount nouns?** Often use no article.

This process is a guide, not a complete list of every English expression. The sections below show the common patterns.

## 9.3 The definite article *the*

Use **the** when the reader can identify which person or thing you mean. The noun may be singular, plural, count, or noncount.

### First mention and later mention

When you introduce a nonspecific singular count noun, use *a/an*. When you mention the same thing again, use *the* if it is now identifiable:

> I borrowed **a book** from the library. **The book** explains basic accounting.

The first mention introduces one book. The later mention points to that same book.

This pattern also works with plural and noncount nouns:

> We interviewed **several applicants**. **The applicants** had different backgrounds.
>
> We collected **information** about the new route. **The information** will help us plan.

### Shared or specific reference

Use *the* when both people know which one is meant, even if it has not been mentioned earlier:

> Please close **the door**. (The door in the room or the one both people can see.)
>
> I left the file on **the desk**. (A particular desk known in this setting.)

A phrase after the noun can identify it:

> **The meeting at 2:00** has moved to Room 301.
>
> Please review **the report you sent yesterday**.

Use *the* with something unique in the relevant context:

> **The sun** is bright today.
>
> **The project manager** will explain the next step. (If the group has one project manager.)

“Unique” depends on context. A workplace may have one manager for a project, while another organization has many.

## 9.4 The indefinite articles *a* and *an*

Use **a** or **an** with a singular count noun when introducing one nonspecific person or thing, or when naming one member of a class.

> I need **a charger**. (Any charger will do.)
>
> She is **an analyst**. (One member of the occupation.)

Use *a/an* only with a singular count noun. Do not use *a/an* directly with a plural or a noncount noun:

> **a question** / **questions** / **some questions**
>
> **advice** / **some advice** / **a piece of advice**

For more on count and noncount nouns, see Chapter 2.

### Choose *a* or *an* by sound

Choose *a* before a consonant sound and *an* before a vowel sound. The first letter does not determine the choice; the first sound does.

| Use *a* before a consonant sound | Use *an* before a vowel sound |
|---|---|
| *a report* | *an email* |
| *a university* (starts with a “y” sound) | *an hour* (the *h* is silent) |
| *a one-time offer* (starts with a “w” sound) | *an MBA* (the letter name starts with a vowel sound) |
| *a useful tool* (starts with a “y” sound) | *an honest answer* (the *h* is silent) |

Say the phrase aloud if you are unsure. Use the sound that begins the next word, even when the next word is an adjective:

> **an interesting proposal**
>
> **a detailed proposal**

## 9.5 The zero article: when no article is used

The **zero article** means that no *a/an* or *the* appears before a noun. It is common when speaking generally about plural count nouns or noncount nouns:

> **Students** need time to revise. (Students in general.)
>
> **Information** is available online. (Information in general.)
>
> **Coffee** helps me stay alert. (Coffee as a general substance.)

Compare a general meaning with a specific meaning:

> **Books** can introduce new ideas. (Books in general.)
>
> **The books** on this shelf are new. (Specific books.)
>
> **Music** can help people focus. (Music in general.)
>
> **The music** in the café is too loud. (Specific music.)

### General statements with singular count nouns

English commonly uses plural nouns for general statements:

> **A smartphone** can be useful for work. (Any one member of the category; singular count noun.)
>
> **Smartphones** can be useful for work. (The category in general.)

Both can make a general statement, but the plural form is often more direct. Do not use a singular count noun by itself for a general statement: *Smartphone can be useful* is not standard; use *a smartphone* or *smartphones*.

## 9.6 Demonstrative determiners

The demonstratives **this, that, these,** and **those** identify people or things by number and, often, distance or time.

| Singular | Plural | Typical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *this* | *these* | Near the speaker, current, or being discussed now |
| *that* | *those* | Farther away, earlier, or less immediate |

> **This document** is ready. (One document close or relevant now.)
>
> **Those forms** are from last year. (Several forms farther away or less immediate.)

These words are determiners when a noun follows: *this document, those forms*. They can be pronouns when they stand alone: *This is ready; those are from last year.*

Use a demonstrative that matches the noun in number: *this report* but *these reports*; *that meeting* but *those meetings*.

## 9.7 Possessive determiners

Possessive determiners show who or what something belongs to or is associated with. They come before a noun:

> **my** notebook
>
> **your** appointment
>
> **his/her/its** location
>
> **our** project
>
> **their** office

The possessive determiner agrees with the owner, not with the noun that follows:

> Dana brought **her laptop**. (The owner is Dana.)
>
> The employees updated **their schedules**. (The owners are the employees.)

Do not use an article before a possessive determiner: *my laptop*, not *the my laptop*. To use a possessive form without a noun, use a possessive pronoun: *This laptop is mine.* Chapter 3 covers these forms in detail.

## 9.8 Quantity determiners

Quantity words tell how many or how much. Choose a form that fits whether the noun is count or noncount.

| Determiner | Count noun | Noncount noun |
|---|---|---|
| *some* | *some questions* | *some information* |
| *any* | *any questions* | *any time* |
| *many* | *many applicants* | — |
| *much* | — | *much work* |
| *few / a few* | *few/a few options* | — |
| *little / a little* | — | *little/a little time* |
| *each / every* | *each student; every student* | — |

### *Some* and *any*

Use *some* often in affirmative statements:

> We have **some questions**.
>
> I need **some time** to review the proposal.

Use *any* often in negatives and questions:

> We do not have **any questions**.
>
> Do you have **any time** this afternoon?

These are common patterns, not absolute rules. Use *some* in an offer or request when you expect or hope the answer will be yes:

> Would you like **some coffee**?
>
> Could I have **some information** about the program?

*Any* can also mean “whichever” or “it does not matter which” in affirmative sentences: *You can choose any seat.*

### *Much* and *many*

Use *many* with plural count nouns and *much* with noncount nouns:

> How **many appointments** are available?
>
> How **much time** do we have?

In everyday affirmative statements, *a lot of* is often more natural than *much*:

> We have **a lot of work** this week.
>
> We have **a lot of meetings** this week.

### *Few/a few* and *little/a little*

Use *few* and *a few* with plural count nouns; use *little* and *a little* with noncount nouns. The article *a* changes the tone:

- **few** / **little** suggest that the amount is small or insufficient;
- **a few** / **a little** mean that at least some amount is available.

> We have **few options** left. (Not many; this may be a problem.)
>
> We have **a few options** left. (Some options remain.)
>
> There is **little time** to make changes. (Almost no time.)
>
> There is **a little time** to make changes. (Some time remains.)

### *Each* and *every*

Use *each* or *every* before a singular count noun:

> **Each applicant** receives a confirmation.
>
> **Every meeting** begins with a short update.

They often refer to all members of a group, but *each* emphasizes individuals one by one, while *every* presents the group as a complete set. The noun and verb are normally singular: *Each form **has** a number; every applicant **receives** an email.* Chapter 11 covers agreement in more detail.

## 9.9 Common article patterns

Article use can depend on whether a place is understood as an activity or institution, or as a specific building. These patterns are common, but expressions can vary by region and context.

### Places, school, and work

Some place words commonly appear without an article when they refer to a usual activity or purpose:

> go to **school** / be at **school**
>
> go to **work** / be at **work**
>
> go **home** / be at **home**
>
> go to **bed** / be in **bed**

Use *the* when you mean a particular building or specific place rather than the activity or routine:

> The parents went to **the school** to meet the principal. (The school building.)
>
> I left my badge at **the office**. (A specific office.)

Not every place follows the same pattern. Learn common expressions as phrases, such as *go to university* in some varieties and *go to college* in others. Names and local usage can differ.

### Meals

Meal names often appear without an article when referring to meals in general or as part of a routine:

> We had **breakfast** at seven.
>
> They are meeting for **lunch**.

Use an article when describing or identifying a particular meal:

> We had **a quick breakfast** before the train.
>
> **The lunch** served at the conference was excellent.

### Languages and academic subjects

Language and subject names usually have no article when used generally:

> She speaks **English** and studies **biology**.

Use an article if another word makes the noun specific or if the noun has a different meaning: *the English spoken in the region; a biology course; the history of medicine*.

## 9.10 Article use varies across languages

Languages do not all use articles in the same way. Some use no articles, some use articles differently, and some use them in places where English does not. As a result, translating an article word for word can lead to errors.

Instead, learn the noun together with an example phrase and the reason for the article:

- *a meeting* — one nonspecific meeting;
- *the meeting* — a particular meeting known to the reader;
- *meetings* — meetings in general;
- *information* — noncount information in general;
- *the information* — particular information already identified.

Articles are a small part of a sentence but often require repeated practice. When you read, notice how a writer introduces a noun and then refers to it again. When you write, ask whether the reader can identify the noun and whether the noun is count or noncount.

## 9.12 Answer key

### A. Edit the project introduction

> **Project introduction: Campus Garden Survey**
>
> Our class is conducting **a survey** about food choices on campus. The survey asks students about **the lunches** they buy and **the information** they use when choosing a meal. We will send **a questionnaire** to students in the biology building and at **the library**. **Each participant** can choose any one of the three time slots for **an interview**. We hope the results will help **the campus dining team** plan **a more useful menu**.

The ten corrections are: add *a* before singular count *survey*; change *a lunches* to *the lunches* because the phrase identifies the lunches the students buy; add *the* before *information* because it refers to information students use in choosing a meal; add *a* before singular count *questionnaire*; add *the* before *biology building*; add *the* before *library*; change *Each participants* to singular *Each participant*; use *an interview* because the next word begins with a vowel sound; add *the* before the specific campus dining team; and add *a* before singular count *menu*.

### B. Choose determiners for shopping and scheduling

1. **an** umbrella — *Umbrella* begins with a vowel sound.
2. **a few** apples — *Apples* is a plural count noun; *a few* means some.
3. **any** printer paper — The sentence is negative; *paper* is noncount here.
4. **some** tea — *Some* is natural in an offer.
5. **a little** time — *Time* is noncount; *a little* means some time remains.
6. **This** appointment … **those** two meetings — *This* matches the singular noun; *those* refers to plural *meetings*.
7. **many** chairs and **much** water — *Chairs* is count; *water* is noncount.
8. **an** hour — *Hour* begins with a vowel sound because the *h* is silent.
9. **a** meeting — The meeting is not yet identified by date or context.
10. **—** Friday — Do not use an article with this deadline expression.

### C. Explain the article choice

1. *A report* introduces one nonspecific report; *the report* refers to that same report after it has been introduced.
2. *Students* is a plural count noun used generally, so it has no article. *The students* identifies a particular group: the students in the speaker's seminar.
3. *Information* is a noncount noun used generally, so it has no article. *The information* identifies specific information on the website.
4. *At work* is a common expression referring to the activity or usual place of employment. *The office* refers to a particular office where the keys were left.
5. *Lunch* refers to a meal as part of a routine, so it has no article. *The lunch* identifies a particular meal served at the conference.

### D. Apply it

Answers will vary. Check that the first mention introduces a nonspecific singular count noun, the later mention identifies it with *the*, general plurals and noncount nouns use no article where appropriate, and each other determiner matches the noun. For example: *Our class started **a project** on campus travel. **The project** compares bus routes. **Students** will complete **this** survey and add **their** comments. We need **some information** about travel times.*

## Chapter summary

Determiners come before nouns to identify or limit them; articles are one kind of determiner. Use *a/an* with a singular count noun when introducing one nonspecific item, and choose between them by the first sound of the next word. Use *the* when the reader can identify the noun from an earlier mention, shared context, or a description. Use no article often with general plural count nouns and noncount nouns. Demonstratives point out which noun; possessive determiners identify whose; quantity determiners show how many or how much. Choose quantity words based on countability, and remember that common patterns such as *some* in offers or no article with *work* and meal names depend on context. Article choices vary across languages, so learn them through repeated examples and reading as well as rules.
