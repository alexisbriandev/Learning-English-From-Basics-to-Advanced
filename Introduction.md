# Introduction

## Welcome

English helps people study, work, share ideas, and take part in everyday life. Learning it is not a single step from “beginner” to “advanced.” Readers bring different strengths: you may read confidently but feel unsure about articles, or speak easily but want to write clearer reports. This book gives you a path through the building blocks of English and room to focus on the skills you need most.

The goal is practical control. You will learn how words work in sentences, how sentences connect, and how to shape paragraphs for readers. Examples and activities draw on college, work, and daily communication. You do not need to master every detail before moving forward. Notice patterns, try them in context, and return to earlier chapters when a later topic raises a question.

## How to use this book

### Follow the progression

The chapters move from smaller language units to larger ones:

1. **Words and their jobs (Chapters 1–10):** Learn the major parts of speech, including nouns, pronouns, verbs, and determiners.
2. **How words work together (Chapters 11–12):** Study agreement, phrases, and clauses.
3. **Sentences and editing (Chapters 13–15):** Build complete sentences, use punctuation, and make context-aware editing decisions.
4. **Paragraphs (Chapter 16):** Organize related sentences into clear, developed writing.

This sequence is useful because later skills depend on earlier ones. For instance, finding the subject helps you check subject-verb agreement, and understanding clauses helps you repair sentence fragments. If a topic feels familiar, use the examples and practice as a quick review. If it feels new, take your time and work through the chapter in order.

### Use the chapter features

Most chapters include some or all of these features:

- **Learning goals** preview what you should be able to do.
- **Plain-language explanations** define technical terms when they first appear.
- **Examples** show how a form works in a sentence or realistic situation.
- **Common challenges** explain errors learners often meet and ways to address them.
- **Practice activities** let you identify, choose, revise, or create language.
- **Answer keys or sample responses** help you check your reasoning. Open-ended writing may have more than one effective answer; compare your choices with the explanation rather than trying to copy a single model exactly.
- **Chapter summaries** highlight ideas to remember and topics to revisit.

When you practice, try the activity before looking at its answer or sample. If your answer differs, ask whether it is grammatical, clear, and suitable for the stated audience. Some exercises have one expected answer; others invite several good solutions.

### Choose a starting point without losing the foundations

You can begin with the quick starting check below, start at Chapter 1, or go directly to a topic you need for a current assignment or task. Use the chapter references in the check to identify a useful place to begin.

If you start later in the book, keep the early chapters available as reference. A paragraph question may turn out to involve verb tense, pronoun reference, or sentence boundaries. Review the relevant foundation when you need it. Skimming a familiar chapter is often enough; skipping a foundation permanently can make later explanations harder to use.

You can also keep a short learning log. Write down a sentence you revised, the pattern you noticed, and one question to check. This makes progress visible and helps you choose what to review next.

## A note on English varieties

English varies across regions, countries, communities, and situations. People may use different vocabulary, pronunciations, spelling conventions, and grammar patterns. They may also shift their language depending on whether they are speaking with friends, writing a class paper, or preparing a workplace report. These varieties are meaningful ways people communicate; a difference from this book's examples does not by itself mean that a speaker's English is deficient.

This book focuses on **standard edited English**: conventions commonly expected in academic and professional writing, such as college assignments, applications, and formal workplace documents. The focus helps you meet the expectations of those settings. It does not claim that there is only one valid form of English.

Where a choice varies, the book will identify the context when possible. For example, collective nouns such as *team* may take singular agreement in common American usage when the group is treated as one unit, while plural agreement is common in some other varieties when the members are emphasized. Words such as *data* may be treated as plural or as a mass noun depending on field and context. **Who** is common in everyday questions where formal writing may use **whom** as an object. Spelling, date formats, punctuation, and title capitalization can also differ by region or style guide.

When you are writing for a course, employer, publication, or application, follow its stated conventions. When none are given, choose a consistent form suited to your readers. The book uses Chicago-based editorial conventions where a consistent choice is needed, while explaining alternatives that learners are likely to encounter.

## Quick starting check

This short check is a way to notice which topics may be useful to review. It is not a test of your intelligence or overall English ability, and it does not assign a proficiency level. Try it without a dictionary first, then use the explanations and chapter references to plan your next steps.

### 1. Word classes

In the sentence below, identify the parts of speech of the bold words.

> **The** **careful** student **revised** her essay **quickly**.

### 2. Sentence boundaries

Which choice is a complete sentence? If the other choice is incomplete, explain what it needs.

**A.** Because the meeting ended early.

**B.** The meeting ended early, so we finished the review.

### 3. Agreement

Choose the verb that completes the sentence:

> The list of required documents **(is / are)** attached.

What is the subject that controls the verb?

### 4. Punctuation

Add punctuation and capitalization:

> after the workshop we emailed our instructor however we forgot to attach the file

### 5. Paragraph organization

Which sentence does not fit a paragraph about preparing for an exam? What could you do with it?

1. I review my notes a few days before the exam.
2. I make a list of questions about topics I do not understand.
3. The campus bookstore sells notebooks in several colors.
4. I use the questions to guide a study session with a classmate.

### Answers and next steps

1. **The** is a determiner (specifically, an article); **careful** is an adjective; **revised** is a verb; **quickly** is an adverb. Review Chapter 1 for the basic jobs of word classes and how a word's role depends on its use.
2. **B** is a complete sentence: it contains two independent clauses joined with *so*. **A** is a dependent clause beginning with *because*; it needs a main clause to complete the thought. Review Chapters 8, 12, and 13 for conjunctions, clauses, and sentence boundaries.
3. **Is** is correct. The subject is **list**, not *documents*; the phrase *of required documents* adds information about the list. Review Chapter 11 for subject-verb agreement.
4. One clear revision is: **After the workshop, we emailed our instructor. However, we forgot to attach the file.** The opening phrase is followed by a comma, and *however* connects the ideas across two sentences. Other punctuation may work if it creates complete, clear sentences. Review Chapter 14 for punctuation and capitalization and Chapter 15 for editing choices.
5. Sentence **3** does not support the exam-preparation point. You could remove it or use it in a separate paragraph about campus bookstore services. The other sentences form a sequence of study actions. Review Chapter 16 for unity, development, and paragraph organization.

Use your answers as directions, not as a score. If one item felt easy, you may be ready to move ahead. If an explanation raised questions, read the referenced chapter and try its practice. You can repeat this check later to notice what has become clearer.

## A final suggestion

Language learning grows through use and review. Read examples aloud, adapt them to situations in your life, and keep track of questions that recur. Aim for writing that is clear, accurate for its purpose, and considerate of its readers. Return to the book as often as you need; building a strong foundation is part of moving forward.
