# Chapter 1. Parts of Speech Overview

## What you will learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- explain what a part of speech is;
- recognize the major word classes and describe their basic jobs;
- identify a word's role by looking at what it connects to or changes in a sentence;
- explain why a word's class cannot always be identified from its meaning or position alone; and
- recognize that the same word can belong to different classes in different sentences.

## 1.1 What is a part of speech?

A **part of speech** is the grammatical role a word plays in a sentence. It tells us how a word works with other words. For example, a word may name a person or thing, describe something, express an action, or connect two ideas.

English has several major word classes: **nouns, pronouns, adjectives, verbs, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions, determiners,** and **interjections**. Each class has a typical job, but a word's class is not permanently fixed. The same spelling can do different jobs in different sentences:

> I **work** at the library on Saturdays.
>
> The **work** is interesting.

In the first sentence, *work* expresses an activity, so it is a **verb**. In the second, *work* names an activity or task, so it is a **noun**. The spelling and general idea are similar; the grammatical job has changed.

### Class, meaning, and position

Three ideas can help you identify a word, but they are not the same:

- **Class** is the word's grammatical category, such as noun or verb.
- **Meaning** is the idea the word communicates.
- **Position** is where the word appears in a sentence.

Meaning can provide a clue, but it is not enough by itself. A word connected to an activity is not always a verb: *study* is a verb in *We study together* and a noun in *The study took two weeks*. Position can also provide a clue, but it is not a complete test. Nouns often follow determiners, as in *the meeting*, but not every word after *the* is a noun: *the careful review* includes the adjective *careful* between the determiner and the noun.

Instead, look at how the word behaves and what it does in the particular sentence. Ask what it names or replaces, what it describes or modifies, and what it connects to other words.

> **A note on grammar terms**
>
> Grammar books sometimes classify a few words differently. For example, some call possessive words such as *my* and *her* adjectives; this book treats them as **determiners** when they come before nouns (*my notes, her essay*). The important skill is to understand the word's job and use one system consistently.

## 1.2 The major word classes

### Nouns: naming words

A **noun** names a person, place, thing, idea, or experience.

- People: *student, manager, neighbor*
- Places: *campus, office, Taipei*
- Things: *phone, report, chair*
- Ideas and experiences: *confidence, research, conversation*

Nouns can appear in many parts of a sentence. They can name who does something (*The **manager** called*), what receives an action (*She reviewed the **report***), or what something is (*He became a **mentor***). Later chapters explain these sentence roles in more detail.

### Pronouns: words that stand in for nouns

A **pronoun** can take the place of a noun or noun phrase. The noun it refers to is called its **antecedent**.

> Maya submitted the form because **she** had finished it.

Here, *she* refers to *Maya* and *it* refers to *the form*. Pronouns help prevent unnecessary repetition, but readers need to know what each pronoun refers to.

Common pronouns include *I, you, he, she, it, we, they, me, them, this,* and *who*. Some words that look similar to pronouns can have a different class depending on what follows. In *This is useful*, *this* stands alone as a pronoun. In *This guide is useful*, *this* comes before and identifies the noun *guide*, so it is a determiner.

### Adjectives: describing or limiting words

An **adjective** describes a noun or pronoun, or helps identify which one or what kind.

> We chose a **quiet** room for the interview.

The adjective *quiet* describes the noun *room*. Adjectives can also follow linking verbs such as *be, seem,* and *become*:

> The room seems **quiet**.

An adjective does not have to express a vivid quality. In *three reports*, the word *three* helps specify the noun; in this book, it is treated as a determiner. The following section explains determiners and how they differ from descriptive adjectives.

### Verbs: actions, events, and states

A **verb** expresses an action, event, or state. A sentence's main verb tells what happens or what is true.

- Action: *They **prepared** for the presentation.*
- Event: *The meeting **ended** at noon.*
- State: *I **understand** the instructions.*

Verbs can change form to show time or to work with helping verbs: *prepare, prepares, prepared, is preparing, has prepared*. A group of words that works together as a verb is called a **verb phrase**. In *has prepared*, *has* helps the main verb *prepared*.

### Adverbs: modifying verbs, adjectives, other adverbs, or clauses

An **adverb** gives information about a verb, an adjective, another adverb, or sometimes a whole clause.

- Verb: She answered **quickly**. (How did she answer?)
- Adjective: The instructions were **very** clear. (*Very* modifies *clear*.)
- Adverb: The bus arrived **quite** late. (*Quite* modifies *late*.)
- Whole clause: **Fortunately**, we saved a copy. (*Fortunately* comments on the whole statement.)

Many adverbs end in *-ly*, but not all do (*often, soon, here, well*). Also, not every word ending in *-ly* is an adverb: *friendly* is usually an adjective. Check the word's job rather than relying only on its ending.

### Prepositions: showing relationships

A **preposition** shows a relationship, often involving time, place, direction, or another connection. It is usually followed by a noun or pronoun, called its **object**. A preposition and its object form a **prepositional phrase**.

> The workshop begins **at noon**.
>
> He placed the folder **on the desk**.
>
> We received a message **from her**.

In these examples, *at, on,* and *from* are prepositions. Their phrases tell when, where, or from whom. Many prepositions are short, common words, including *in, near, with, about, before,* and *through*.

### Conjunctions: connecting words and ideas

A **conjunction** connects words, phrases, or clauses. A **clause** is a group of words organized around a subject and a verb; chapters 12 and 13 explore clauses and sentences more fully.

- Connecting words: tea **and** coffee
- Connecting phrases: in the morning **or** after work
- Connecting clauses: I sent the file, **but** I forgot the attachment.

Common conjunctions include *and, but, or, so, because, although,* and *if*. The relationship matters: *but* signals contrast, while *because* signals a reason.

### Determiners: introducing or limiting nouns

A **determiner** comes before a noun to show which one, whose, how many, or how much. Determiners help a reader identify or understand the noun.

- Articles: **a** meeting, **an** email, **the** schedule
- Demonstratives: **this** room, **those** files
- Possessives: **my** class, **her** desk, **their** project
- Quantity words: **some** time, **many** questions, **each** student
- Numbers: **two** colleagues, **first** attempt

The three articles are *a, an,* and *the*. *A* and *an* usually introduce one nonspecific countable noun (*a question, an answer*); *the* points to a specific or identifiable noun (*the answer we discussed*). The choice between *a* and *an* depends on the first sound of the next word: *a university* begins with a consonant sound, while *an hour* begins with a vowel sound.

Determiners are not the same as adjectives in this book. In *those useful notes*, *those* is a determiner that identifies which notes, *useful* is an adjective that describes them, and *notes* is the noun.

### Interjections: expressing a reaction

An **interjection** is a short word or expression that shows a reaction or helps manage an interaction.

> **Oh**, I see what you mean.
>
> **Great!** The room is available.

Interjections often appear outside the main grammatical structure of a sentence. Their punctuation and tone depend on the situation. *Wow!* may fit a conversation with a friend, while a formal report would usually express the reaction in a more measured way.

## 1.3 See the classes working together

Consider this sentence:

> **The careful student revised her essay quickly.**

| Word | Class | Job in this sentence |
|---|---|---|
| *The* | determiner (article) | Identifies which student. |
| *careful* | adjective | Describes the student. |
| *student* | noun | Names the person doing the action. |
| *revised* | verb | Tells what the student did. |
| *her* | determiner (possessive) | Identifies whose essay. |
| *essay* | noun | Names what was revised. |
| *quickly* | adverb | Tells how the student revised it. |

The sentence works because the words have different jobs and fit together in a meaningful pattern. Notice that *her* is not replacing a noun here: it comes before *essay* and identifies whose essay. Compare *The essay is hers*, where *hers* stands alone as a pronoun.

Here is another example with more connections:

> **After class, the tutor and I reviewed the difficult questions together.**

| Word or phrase | Class or structure | Job |
|---|---|---|
| *After* | preposition | Begins a phrase that tells when. |
| *class* | noun | Object of the preposition *after*. |
| *the* | determiner (article) | Identifies *tutor*. |
| *tutor* | noun | Names one person in the subject. |
| *and* | conjunction | Connects two subject pronouns. |
| *I* | pronoun | Names the other person in the subject. |
| *reviewed* | verb | Tells what the tutor and I did. |
| *the* | determiner (article) | Identifies *questions*. |
| *difficult* | adjective | Describes the questions. |
| *questions* | noun | Names what we reviewed. |
| *together* | adverb | Tells how we reviewed them. |

The phrase *after class* gives time information, but it is not a complete clause: it does not say who did what. The main clause is *the tutor and I reviewed the difficult questions together*. You will study phrases and clauses in detail later.

## 1.4 A practical method for identifying a word's class

When you are unsure about a word, use these steps. Start with the sentence, not an isolated dictionary definition.

1. **Read the whole sentence.** A word's role depends on its context.
2. **Find the main verb.** Ask what action, event, or state the sentence expresses.
3. **Look for naming or replacing words.** Does the word name a person, place, thing, or idea (noun)? Does it stand in for one (pronoun)?
4. **Check for words that come before and identify nouns.** A determiner often signals which one, whose, or how many. An adjective may describe the noun.
5. **Ask what is being modified.** A describing word that modifies a noun or pronoun is often an adjective. A word that modifies a verb, adjective, adverb, or full clause is often an adverb.
6. **Look at connections.** Does the word begin a phrase that relates to another word (preposition)? Does it join words or ideas (conjunction)?
7. **Use position as a clue, not a verdict.** Then reread the sentence to see whether the proposed class fits its meaning and structure.

### Try the method: *work*

> **I work** at the library today.

The sentence's main verb is *work*. It follows the subject *I* and expresses what I do. Here, *work* is a verb.

> **The work** is challenging.

Here, *is* is the verb. *Work* follows the determiner *the* and names an activity or task. Here, *work* is a noun.

> We need **work clothes** for the event.

Here, *clothes* is the noun. *Work* helps specify the kind of clothes, so it modifies a noun and functions as a noun modifier (often called a **noun adjunct**). This example shows why a word cannot be classified reliably by spelling alone. The chapters ahead focus on the most common patterns before exploring such details more deeply.

### Another word with more than one job: *email*

> Please **email** the updated schedule.

*Email* is a verb: it tells someone what action to take.

> I sent an **email** with the updated schedule.

*Email* is a noun: it names a message. The determiner *an* is a useful clue, but the word's relationship to the rest of the sentence confirms its role.

## 1.5 Common trouble spots

### Treating every word as if it has one permanent class

A dictionary may list several classes for a word because people use it in different ways. Identify the class in the sentence you are reading, not for the spelling in general.

### Identifying a word only by its meaning

Words about actions are not always verbs, and words about things are not always nouns. *A quick run* uses *run* as a noun; *They run every morning* uses it as a verb. The word's connections reveal its job.

### Identifying a word only by its location

Words in the same position may have different classes, and a class may appear in more than one position. In *a fast train*, *fast* is an adjective describing *train*. In *The train travels fast*, *fast* is an adverb modifying *travels*. Look at what the word modifies.

### Confusing determiners and pronouns

Some words can belong to either class. A determiner comes before a noun (*those messages*); a pronoun stands in for a noun phrase (*those are important*). Ask whether a noun follows the word.

### Expecting every adverb to end in *-ly*

Many do, but words such as *often, soon,* and *well* can be adverbs. Some *-ly* words, such as *friendly*, are adjectives. Check the job.

Answers will vary. Check that the sentences are complete and that each label matches the word's job in its sentence. For example, in *The new analyst reviewed the report carefully before lunch, but she sent it later*, *The* and *the* are determiners; *new* is an adjective; *analyst, report,* and *lunch* are nouns; *reviewed* and *sent* are verbs; *carefully* and *later* are adverbs; *before* is a preposition; *but* is a conjunction; and *she* and *it* are pronouns.

Answers will vary. A successful response uses the same spelling in two different grammatical roles and explains each role from context. For example: *I **text** my teammate after class* (verb); *I sent a **text** to my teammate* (noun).

## Chapter summary

Parts of speech describe the jobs words do in sentences. Nouns name; pronouns stand in for nouns or noun phrases; adjectives describe or limit nouns and pronouns; verbs express actions, events, and states; adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, adverbs, or clauses; prepositions show relationships; conjunctions connect; determiners introduce or limit nouns; and interjections express reactions. A word's class depends on its use in context, so meaning and position are clues rather than guarantees. To identify a word, look at what it names or replaces, what it modifies, and how it connects to surrounding words.
